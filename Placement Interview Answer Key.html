How would you reduce traffic congestion in Bangalore?

Asked as: "How will you prevent or solve Bangalore traffic problems." This is a structured-thinking question, not a civil engineering question. They want to see if you scope a vague problem, find root causes, and propose something measurable.

Open by scoping it

"Let me narrow it — I'll focus on peak-hour congestion on the IT corridors, Outer Ring Road, Whitefield and Electronic City, because that's where the largest share of delay-minutes sits. And I'll define success as reducing average peak commute time and increasing person-throughput, not vehicle-throughput."

That one sentence already separates you from candidates who jump straight to "build more flyovers."

Root causes

  • Radial road network with very few complete ring roads, so cross-city trips funnel through the centre.
  • Vehicle growth outpacing road length — roughly 1.2 crore registered vehicles against a road network that has barely grown.
  • Employment concentration. Lakhs of people travelling to the same 3–4 tech clusters, arriving in the same 90-minute window.
  • Low public transport share. Limited metro coverage until recently, an under-sized BMTC fleet, and weak last-mile connectivity.
  • Carriageway loss to illegal parking, encroached footpaths pushing pedestrians onto the road, construction barricades, and waterlogging in monsoon.
  • Operational issues — unsynchronised signals, poor junction geometry, no lane discipline, breakdowns blocking lanes.

Solutions, grouped by lever and timeline

Demand side (months, cheapest): staggered office hours agreed with large employers on ORR so the peak spreads across three hours instead of one; two mandated work-from-home days; employer-run shuttle buses with a parking cap per campus; priced parking in CBD; eventually congestion pricing on the worst corridors in peak hours.

Supply side (years, expensive): accelerate metro phases and the suburban rail project, double the BMTC fleet, and build dedicated bus-priority lanes — a bus lane moves far more people per hour per lane than mixed traffic. Fix the last mile with feeder autos, e-bikes and safe footpaths, because most people abandon the metro over the last 1.5 km.

Operations (weeks, high ROI): adaptive signal control that responds to live queue length instead of a fixed timer, junction redesign at the top bottlenecks, one-way pairs, freight movement restricted to night hours, and rapid incident clearance tow teams.

Land use (decade): transit-oriented development and decentralised employment hubs so people stop travelling across the city in the first place.

The part where you show you're a tech candidate

  • Fuse GPS floating-car data, CCTV feeds, FASTag and bus AVL data to rank the top 50 bottleneck junctions by delay-minutes, so spending follows evidence.
  • Build a traffic digital twin in a simulator like SUMO and test each intervention virtually before spending a rupee on it.
  • Use reinforcement learning or at minimum queue-responsive control for signal timing across a corridor, not per junction.
  • Computer vision on existing cameras for automated enforcement of lane discipline and no-parking zones.
  • Short-term congestion prediction to pre-position traffic marshals and tow trucks.

Metrics and trade-offs

Track average peak travel time on the top corridors, average speed, public-transport modal share, and buses per hour per corridor. Then name the trade-offs honestly: congestion pricing is regressive unless paired with cheap transit, bus lanes are politically unpopular because they visibly take a lane from cars, and metro construction makes congestion worse before it makes it better.

Close like this: "There's no single fix. The highest return in the next six months is bus priority plus staggered work hours; the only permanent fix is shifting mode share from private vehicles to transit."

Design a chatbot for BookMyShow. How would you build it without an LLM, and what changes with one?

Asked as: "Book my show — chatbot with LLM, without LLM." They want the classical NLU pipeline, the modern LLM design, and a clear view of the trade-off.

First, define what the bot must do

Search movies, check showtimes for a city and date, book and cancel tickets, check refund status, apply offers, and answer policy FAQs. Booking is a transactional flow that touches money — that single fact drives most of the design.

Without an LLM — the classical pipeline

  1. Preprocessing: tokenise, lowercase, spell-correct, handle Hinglish transliteration.
  2. Intent classification: a fixed intent set (search_movie, get_showtimes, book_ticket, cancel_booking, refund_status, offers, greeting, fallback). Implemented with regex plus TF-IDF and an SVM or logistic regression, or a small fine-tuned BERT classifier. Confidence threshold below which you fall back.
  3. Entity extraction / slot filling: movie name, city, date, theatre, showtime, seat count, seat class. Gazetteer lookup and fuzzy matching for movie and theatre names, date parsers for "tomorrow" and "this weekend", a CRF or spaCy NER for the rest.
  4. Dialogue manager: a finite state machine over a frame. The bot keeps asking for whichever slot is still empty, and moves state only when the frame is complete.
  5. Backend integration: call the real APIs — catalogue, seat availability, hold, payment, booking.
  6. Response generation: templates with placeholders. Deterministic wording, legally safe.
  7. Fallback: after two failed turns, hand off to a human agent.

Strengths: predictable, auditable, cheap, millisecond latency, never invents a refund policy. Weaknesses: brittle to paraphrase, can't handle "book me something fun near Koramangala tonight, two people", needs labelled data per intent, and the intent list becomes unmaintainable as it grows.

With an LLM

  • The LLM replaces the NLU layer entirely — no intent classifier, no slot-filling model. It understands paraphrase, multi-intent, code-mixed language and context carry-over out of the box.
  • Tool / function calling is the core: define searchMovies(query, city), getShowtimes(movieId, date, city), holdSeats(showId, seats), createBooking(holdId, paymentToken), getRefundStatus(bookingId). The model decides which to call and with what arguments; the real system still executes them.
  • Structured output: force the booking parameters into a JSON schema so downstream code is type-safe.
  • RAG over policy docs for cancellation rules, convenience fees and offer terms, so the answers are grounded and citable instead of memorised.
  • Guardrails: the model is never allowed to state a price, seat availability or refund amount from its own head — every such number must come from a tool result. Irreversible steps (payment, cancellation) require an explicit confirmation turn.
  • Safety and ops: PII redaction before logging, prompt-injection defence on anything user-supplied, a small cheap model for routing and a larger one only for hard turns, streaming for perceived latency, response caching, and an offline evaluation set of a few hundred real conversations that you re-run on every prompt change.

The answer that actually wins

"In production I'd use a hybrid. The LLM handles understanding, disambiguation and small talk, but the moment the user enters the booking funnel I hand control to a deterministic state machine, because I don't want a probabilistic model driving a payment flow. If the LLM provider is down, the old intent classifier is the fallback path so the funnel still works."

Explain a RAG pipeline end to end. Why choose RAG over fine-tuning?

Asked as: "RAG pipelines." Give the two halves — offline ingestion and online query — then evaluation, then failure modes. That structure alone signals you've built one.

What problem it solves

An LLM only knows what was in its training data, it can't cite sources, and it can't see your private documents. RAG fixes that by retrieving relevant passages at query time and putting them into the prompt, so the model reasons over supplied evidence instead of memory.

Stage 1 — Ingestion (offline)

  1. Load and parse the sources: PDFs, Confluence, tickets, HTML. This is the stage that quietly ruins most pipelines — bad PDF parsing destroys tables and multi-column layouts.
  2. Clean: strip boilerplate, headers, footers, navigation.
  3. Chunk. Fixed-size (say 500 tokens with 10–15% overlap) is the baseline; recursive splitting on headings and paragraphs is better; semantic chunking or parent-child (retrieve a small chunk, feed the larger parent to the model) is better still. Chunk boundaries decide retrieval quality more than the embedding model does.
  4. Attach metadata: source, title, section, date, access-control tags, URL. You need these for filtering and citations.
  5. Embed each chunk with an embedding model into a dense vector.
  6. Index into a vector store (FAISS, pgvector, Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, Qdrant) using HNSW or IVF, and build a keyword index (BM25) alongside it.

Stage 2 — Query time (online)

  1. Query understanding: rewrite the question using conversation history so follow-ups like "and for the second one?" resolve; optionally decompose multi-part questions, or generate a hypothetical answer and embed that (HyDE).
  2. Retrieve: dense vector search plus BM25 keyword search, fused — hybrid retrieval, because dense search misses exact identifiers, error codes and product names. Pull a wide top-k, around 20–50. Apply metadata filters here (date ranges, and crucially the user's access permissions).
  3. Rerank: a cross-encoder scores each candidate against the query properly and you keep the top 4–6. This is usually the single biggest quality win for the least effort.
  4. Assemble context: order the passages, deduplicate, tag each with a source id, and stay within the token budget. Put the most important passages at the start and end — models attend less to the middle.
  5. Generate: a prompt that says answer only from the provided context, cite the source id for every claim, and say you don't know if the context is insufficient.
  6. Post-process: verify citations exist, optionally run a groundedness check, and render the sources in the UI.

Evaluation — have an answer ready, most candidates don't

  • Retrieval: recall@k, precision@k, MRR, nDCG against a labelled question-to-chunk set.
  • Generation: faithfulness or groundedness (is every claim supported by context), answer relevance, context precision and recall. The RAGAS framework covers these.
  • System: p95 latency, cost per query, deflection rate, and human thumbs-up rate in production.

Failure modes and fixes

SymptomLikely causeFix
Right document, wrong chunkChunking splits the answerOverlap, heading-aware splitting, parent-child retrieval
Retrieves nothing relevantDomain vocabulary mismatchHybrid BM25, query rewriting, fine-tuned embeddings
Answer contradicts docsModel falls back on parametric memoryStricter prompt, groundedness check, lower temperature
Stale answersIndex not refreshedIncremental re-indexing, recency metadata boost
Multi-hop questions failSingle-shot retrievalQuery decomposition or agentic / iterative retrieval
Leaks other teams' dataNo permission filteringAccess tags in metadata, filter before retrieval

RAG vs fine-tuning

RAG changes what the model knows; fine-tuning changes how the model behaves. Use RAG for facts that change, for anything needing citations, and for per-user access control — updating means re-indexing a document, not retraining. Use fine-tuning for tone, output format, domain jargon and shorter prompts. They're complementary, and in practice you start with RAG because it's cheaper to iterate on.

What is an AI agent, and how is it different from a plain LLM call?

Asked as: "AI agent." Define it sharply, name the components, then say where agents go wrong — the last part is what separates a candidate who has built one from one who has read about them.

Definition

An AI agent is a system where an LLM decides, in a loop, which actions to take to achieve a goal — calling tools, observing the results, and choosing what to do next — until the goal is met or a stopping condition triggers. The key word is decides. A plain LLM call maps input to text; an agent controls its own process.

The loop

Goal → the model reasons about the next step → it calls a tool → the environment returns an observation → the model re-plans with that observation in context → repeat → finish. This is the ReAct pattern: interleaved reasoning and acting.

Components

  • Model — the reasoning engine, with a system prompt defining role, constraints and stopping rules.
  • Tools — functions with typed schemas: search, database query, send email, run code, call an internal API. Tool descriptions matter as much as the prompt; a badly described tool gets misused.
  • Memory — short-term (the running context, summarised as it grows) and long-term (a vector store or database of past interactions and learned facts).
  • Planning — implicit step-by-step, explicit plan-then-execute, or reflection where the agent critiques its own output and retries.
  • Orchestration — the runtime that enforces max iterations, timeouts, retries, and a cost budget.
  • Guardrails — permission scoping per tool, human approval before irreversible actions, sandboxed code execution, output validation.
  • Observability — tracing every step, because when an agent fails you need to see which step derailed.

Common patterns

  • ReAct — reason, act, observe. The default.
  • Reflection / Reflexion — the agent evaluates its own result and iterates.
  • Multi-agent — a supervisor delegating to specialist workers, each with a narrow toolset. Useful for parallel, separable subtasks; it multiplies cost and failure modes, so don't reach for it first.
  • Code-as-action — the agent writes and runs code rather than calling one tool at a time.

A concrete example to have ready

A support-triage agent: given a ticket, it searches the knowledge base, queries the order database for the customer's last order, checks the shipment API, drafts a reply, and either sends it or escalates. Tools are read-only except sendReply, which requires human approval for the first few weeks. Max 8 iterations, 30-second budget, full trace logged.

Why agents are hard — say this part out loud

  • Compounding error. 95% reliability per step is 60% over ten steps. Keep chains short and verify intermediate results.
  • Cost and latency grow with every loop iteration.
  • Prompt injection through tool output — a web page or email the agent reads can contain instructions. Treat all tool output as untrusted data.
  • Non-determinism makes testing hard; you need trajectory-level evaluation, not just final-answer checks.
  • Loops and thrash — always cap iterations and detect repeated identical actions.
Best closing line: "Use the simplest thing that works. Most problems people give to agents are actually fixed workflows in disguise, and a workflow is cheaper, faster and testable. Reach for an agent only when the steps genuinely can't be known in advance."

What are LLM workflows, and when do you pick a workflow over an agent?

Asked as: "Workflows for AI." This is the companion to the agent question — answer it as the disciplined, production-minded alternative.

The distinction

In a workflow, the steps are defined in code and the LLM fills in specific slots. In an agent, the LLM decides the steps. Workflows are predictable, cheap, testable and auditable. Agents are flexible and expensive. Most production systems are workflows with one agentic step inside them.

The five patterns worth naming

  • Prompt chaining — break a task into sequential calls, each using the previous output, with programmatic checks between them. Example: extract data → validate → summarise. Use when the decomposition is obvious.
  • Routing — a small classifier call sends the input down one of several specialised paths. Example: a support query routed to billing, technical or refunds, each with its own prompt and tools. Lets you use a cheap model for easy paths.
  • Parallelisation — two flavours. Sectioning splits a task into independent subtasks run concurrently and joined at the end. Voting runs the same task several times and takes consensus, which is useful for code review or content moderation where recall matters.
  • Orchestrator-workers — a lead call breaks the task into subtasks dynamically and delegates each to a worker call. Use when the number of subtasks isn't known in advance, such as editing across an unknown set of files.
  • Evaluator-optimiser — one call produces, another critiques against explicit criteria, and it loops. Works well when you have clear evaluation criteria and iteration genuinely improves the output, such as translation or long-form writing.

A worked example

Invoice processing: OCR the document → extract fields into a strict JSON schema → validate against the purchase order in the ERP → if totals match and amount is under a threshold, post automatically; otherwise route to a human queue with the discrepancy highlighted → write the outcome back and log everything.

Notice: exactly one LLM step does the hard part (extraction), and everything else is ordinary software. That's what a good production design looks like.

Production concerns to mention

  • Determinism where it matters — low temperature, strict output schemas, and validation on every model output before it moves downstream.
  • Reliability — retries with backoff, idempotency keys so a retry doesn't double-charge, dead-letter queues, and timeouts.
  • Cost control — route easy cases to smaller models, cache repeated prompts, and batch where latency allows.
  • Versioning and evaluation — prompts are code: version them, and run a regression eval suite before any prompt change ships.
  • Human in the loop at the steps with real-world consequences.
  • Monitoring — latency, token spend, schema-validation failure rate, and drift in output distribution.
  • Tooling — LangGraph or similar for graph-structured LLM flows; Temporal, Airflow or Step Functions when you need durable execution across long-running steps.

What's the difference between a vulnerability, a threat, an attack and a risk?

Asked as: "Cybersecurity questions like vulnerabilities, threats and attacks." Define each, then tie them together with one example — the example is what they're actually testing.

The four definitions

TermMeaningExample
AssetAnything of value you're protectingThe customer database
VulnerabilityA weakness in a system, process or personA login form that concatenates user input into SQL
ThreatA potential cause of harm — an actor or event that could exploit the weaknessA financially motivated attacker group; also a disgruntled insider, or a flood
ExploitThe specific technique or code that takes advantage of the vulnerabilityThe crafted payload ' OR 1=1 --
AttackThe actual act of using the exploit against the assetDumping the users table at 2 a.m.
RiskLikelihood × impact — the business exposureHigh: 2 million PII records, regulatory penalty, brand damage

The relationship in one line: Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact. Remove any one factor and the risk collapses. You usually can't remove the threat — attackers exist regardless — so security work concentrates on removing vulnerabilities and reducing impact.

Supporting vocabulary they may probe

  • CIA triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability. Every security control serves at least one. A DDoS attacks availability; SQL injection attacks confidentiality and integrity.
  • Attack surface — every point where an attacker can interact with the system. Attack vector — the specific path used, such as a phishing email or an exposed API.
  • Zero-day — a vulnerability with no patch available yet, because the vendor doesn't know or hasn't fixed it.
  • CVE / CWE / CVSS — CVE is the identifier for a specific known vulnerability, CWE is the class of weakness, CVSS is the 0–10 severity score.
  • Control types — preventive (firewall, input validation), detective (IDS, logging), corrective (backups, patching). Also administrative, technical and physical.
  • Principles — defence in depth, least privilege, fail securely, zero trust, no security through obscurity.
  • Threat modelling — STRIDE: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege.

The attack categories worth listing

Web: injection (SQL, command, LDAP), XSS, CSRF, SSRF, IDOR, broken authentication, security misconfiguration. Network: MITM, spoofing, sniffing, DDoS, DNS poisoning. Human: phishing, spear phishing, vishing, pretexting, baiting. Malware: ransomware, trojans, worms, rootkits, keyloggers. Credential: brute force, credential stuffing, password spraying, rainbow tables. Supply chain: compromised dependencies and build pipelines. Passive vs active is a useful split — sniffing is passive, tampering is active.

Explain DDoS — the types, how you detect it, and how you mitigate it

Asked as: "Explain DDoS." Anyone can define it. Score marks on the three-layer taxonomy, detection signals, and admitting you mitigate rather than prevent.

Definition

A Denial of Service attack makes a service unavailable to legitimate users by exhausting a finite resource — bandwidth, connection table, CPU, memory or a database connection pool. It's Distributed when traffic comes from thousands of compromised machines (a botnet, often hijacked IoT devices, as with Mirai), which is what makes it hard: you can't just block one IP, and the traffic looks like it comes from everywhere.

Three layers — this is the part to nail

TypeHow it worksExamplesMeasured in
Volumetric (L3/L4)Saturate the target's bandwidthUDP flood, ICMP flood, DNS / NTP / memcached amplificationGbps
Protocol / state exhaustion (L3/L4)Exhaust connection state on servers, load balancers and firewallsSYN flood, ACK flood, fragmented packet attacksPackets per second
Application layer (L7)Send few but expensive requests that look legitimateHTTP GET/POST flood, Slowloris, cache-busting search queriesRequests per second

Amplification and reflection is worth explaining properly because it's a favourite follow-up: the attacker sends a small query to an open public server (DNS, NTP, memcached) with the source IP spoofed to be the victim's. The server's much larger reply goes to the victim. A 60-byte request producing a 3,000-byte response gives an amplification factor of 50×, so a modest botnet generates enormous traffic, and the victim sees legitimate servers attacking it.

SYN flood: TCP's three-way handshake reserves state after SYN. The attacker sends SYNs and never sends the final ACK, filling the backlog queue with half-open connections until legitimate handshakes are refused.

Slowloris: opens many connections and sends partial HTTP headers very slowly, just fast enough to avoid timeouts, holding every worker thread hostage with almost no bandwidth.

Detection signals

  • A sharp spike in traffic well outside the established baseline, especially from unusual geographies or a handful of ASNs.
  • Huge numbers of connections in SYN_RECV state, or a connection count far exceeding concurrent user count.
  • Response time and 5xx errors climbing while application CPU looks oddly idle (pointing at network or LB saturation).
  • Many requests to the same expensive endpoint, identical user agents, or requests with cache-busting random query strings.
  • Flow telemetry (NetFlow / sFlow) showing packets-per-second anomalies, and alerts from the CDN or WAF.

Mitigation

  • Absorb upstream. Route traffic through a scrubbing provider or CDN with anycast, so attack traffic is spread across many global points of presence and filtered before it reaches your origin. Then hide the origin IP so it can't be attacked directly.
  • Rate limiting per IP, per session, per API token, with stricter limits on expensive endpoints.
  • SYN cookies so the server doesn't allocate state until the handshake completes; tune backlog and connection timeouts.
  • WAF and bot management — signature rules, JavaScript challenges, CAPTCHA, device fingerprinting, behavioural scoring for L7 floods.
  • Caching and CDN offload so most requests never touch the origin; autoscaling to ride out moderate spikes.
  • Network controls — blackhole or remotely triggered blackhole routing as a last resort, BGP FlowSpec for surgical filtering, geo-blocking, and closing unused UDP services.
  • Anti-spoofing — BCP38 ingress filtering at the network edge reduces reflection attacks at source.
  • Preparation — a written runbook, your ISP's emergency contact, and a tested failover plan. Response speed matters more than cleverness during an incident.
Nuance that impresses: you don't prevent a DDoS, you survive it. And a DDoS is sometimes a smokescreen — while the SOC is firefighting availability, the real intrusion happens elsewhere. Always check for that afterwards.

How would you look for vulnerabilities in a system? And how would you know if a database has been hit by SQL injection?

Asked as: "How will u look for vulnerabilities, give example. How will u get to know if a db is affected by SQL injections." Two parts — answer the methodology first, then get very specific on SQLi.

Part A — a methodology, not a tool list

  1. Authorisation and scope first. Say this out loud. Written permission, defined scope, defined test window. Testing without it is a crime, and interviewers notice when you skip it.
  2. Reconnaissance. Build an asset inventory: subdomain enumeration, port and service scanning with nmap, technology fingerprinting, exposed cloud storage, leaked credentials, and forgotten staging environments — which are usually the weakest link.
  3. Automated scanning. Infrastructure with Nessus, OpenVAS or Qualys. Web applications with Burp Suite or OWASP ZAP. Containers and images with Trivy. Cloud configuration with a posture scanner. Dependencies with SCA — Snyk, Dependabot, OWASP Dependency-Check — since most real-world exposure is an old library, not clever code.
  4. Static analysis (SAST) on source: Semgrep, SonarQube, CodeQL. Plus secret scanning with gitleaks, because hard-coded keys in git history are everywhere.
  5. Manual testing. Tools can't find business-logic flaws: buying at a negative quantity, skipping a payment step, changing an order id to see someone else's invoice, race conditions on coupon redemption.
  6. Triage and verify. Reproduce each finding, discard false positives, score with CVSS, and prioritise by exploitability and asset value rather than by raw score.
  7. Report, remediate, retest. A finding isn't closed until the fix is verified, and ideally a regression test exists.

A concrete example — testing a login page

"Give an example" usually means "pick one feature and show me you'd be systematic." Take a login page and check: SQL injection in both fields; user enumeration via different error messages or response timing for valid and invalid usernames; rate limiting and account lockout for brute force and credential stuffing; password policy and how passwords are stored; session cookie flags (HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite) and whether the session id rotates after login; JWT weaknesses such as the none algorithm or a weak signing secret; the password-reset flow, including token entropy and whether tokens expire; missing multi-factor authentication; verbose error messages leaking stack traces; and missing security headers.

Part B — is the application injectable?

  • Read the code first. The fastest check is grepping for string concatenation into a query. "SELECT * FROM users WHERE id = " + userInput is the bug; parameterised queries are the absence of it.
  • Error-based probe: submit a single quote. A database syntax error or a 500 means input reaches the parser unescaped.
  • Boolean-based blind: compare responses for ' AND '1'='1 against ' AND '1'='2. Different pages for a logically true vs false condition confirm injection even with errors suppressed.
  • Union-based: find the column count with ORDER BY n until it errors, then append a UNION SELECT to pull data into the rendered output.
  • Time-based blind: when the response never changes, inject a delay — SLEEP(5) in MySQL, pg_sleep(5) in Postgres, WAITFOR DELAY in SQL Server — and measure the response time. A consistent five-second delay is proof.
  • Out-of-band: make the database perform a DNS or HTTP lookup to a domain you control; useful when the response gives nothing away.
  • Tools: Burp Scanner and sqlmap automate all of the above — with authorisation only.

Part B continued — has the database already been attacked?

This is the half most candidates miss. Detection is a log and monitoring question.

  • Web server and WAF logs: search for injection signatures in parameters — UNION SELECT, ' OR '1'='1, --, information_schema, xp_cmdshell, LOAD_FILE, INTO OUTFILE, and their URL-encoded forms like %27. A burst of these from one IP or a scanner user-agent is the signature of sqlmap.
  • A spike in 500 errors or database syntax errors in application logs, especially clustered on one endpoint and one parameter.
  • Database audit and slow-query logs: queries from the application's account touching system catalogs — information_schema.tables, mysql.user, pg_catalog — are almost never legitimate application behaviour. Also look for unusually long-running queries and repeated sleep calls.
  • Data exfiltration signals: a sudden jump in rows returned or bytes sent, a single query pulling an entire table, or large outbound responses at odd hours.
  • Integrity and privilege changes: new database users, granted privileges, modified stored procedures, dropped or altered tables, unexpected files written to disk.
  • Host signals: outbound connections from the database server, new scheduled tasks, or a web shell appearing in the web root.
  • Tooling: database activity monitoring, IDS/IPS alerts, and a SIEM correlating web logs with database logs so a suspicious request and a suspicious query are linked into one incident.

The fix — always end here

Parameterised queries / prepared statements are the actual answer, because they separate code from data so input can never change the query's structure. Supporting controls: an ORM used with bound parameters (not raw string interpolation), allow-list input validation, a least-privilege database account with no DDL or file permissions, generic error messages in production, a WAF as a compensating control, and stored procedures only if they themselves don't concatenate. Escaping is a weak last resort.

// Vulnerable
String q = "SELECT * FROM users WHERE email = '" + email + "'";

// Safe — the driver sends the query and the value separately
PreparedStatement ps = conn.prepareStatement(
    "SELECT * FROM users WHERE email = ?");
ps.setString(1, email);

How do you intercept HTTP and HTTPS traffic? Why is HTTPS harder?

Asked as: "How will u intercept HTTP and HTTPS requests." They want to hear that HTTPS interception is a deliberate, consented man-in-the-middle using a trusted certificate — and that you know it's only legal on systems you own or are authorised to test.

HTTP — trivial, because it's plaintext

  • Browser DevTools network tab for your own traffic.
  • Packet capture with Wireshark or tcpdump — you can read full requests and responses off the wire.
  • An intercepting proxy — Burp Suite, OWASP ZAP, mitmproxy, Fiddler or Charles. You point the browser, OS or app at the proxy (typically 127.0.0.1:8080), and every request passes through where you can view, modify, replay or drop it.
  • On a shared network, ARP spoofing or a rogue access point puts you on-path — lab and authorised-test territory only.

HTTPS — why the same approach fails

HTTPS is HTTP inside a TLS tunnel. Once the handshake finishes, a packet capture shows only encrypted records. You can see the destination IP, the port, the size and timing of packets, and the hostname in the SNI field of the ClientHello, but not a single header, cookie or body byte. TLS also authenticates the server with a certificate signed by a CA your device trusts, so a naive man-in-the-middle triggers a certificate warning.

How interception actually works

You make the proxy a trusted man-in-the-middle by splitting one TLS connection into two:

  1. The client connects to the proxy, which presents a certificate for the requested hostname that it generates on the fly.
  2. That certificate is signed by the proxy's own CA, which you have deliberately installed into the device's trusted root store. Because it's trusted, the client accepts it with no warning.
  3. The proxy decrypts the client's traffic, shows it to you in plaintext, then opens a second, normal TLS connection to the real server and forwards the request.
  4. Responses travel back the same way. You sit in the middle with full read and write access.

Concretely with Burp: set the browser proxy to Burp, visit http://burp, download the CA certificate, install it in the OS or browser trusted root store, and the traffic becomes readable. On Android you additionally have to place the certificate in the system store (rooted device or emulator) or ship a debug build whose network security config trusts user certificates, because since Android 7 apps ignore user-installed CAs by default.

What still blocks you — and what it shows you know

  • Certificate pinning. The app ships the expected certificate or public key and rejects anything else, so your proxy CA doesn't help. Bypassing it needs runtime instrumentation (Frida / objection) or patching the app — strictly on apps you own or are authorised to test.
  • HSTS stops downgrade-to-HTTP tricks and removes the click-through warning.
  • Mutual TLS requires a client certificate the proxy doesn't have.
  • TLS 1.3 and Encrypted Client Hello shrink what's visible even before interception — with ECH, even SNI stops leaking the hostname.
  • Certificate Transparency and pinning in browsers make silent interception on the public internet detectable.

Decrypting without a man in the middle

For your own traffic, set the SSLKEYLOGFILE environment variable before launching Chrome or Firefox; the browser writes the TLS session keys to that file, and Wireshark can be pointed at it to decrypt the capture. This is the cleanest method for debugging your own machine. The old approach of handing Wireshark the server's private key only works with RSA key exchange — with modern ECDHE ciphersuites, forward secrecy means the private key can't decrypt past sessions.

Say this: "Interception is only legitimate on traffic you own or have written authorisation to test. Enterprises do the same thing at scale — a TLS-inspecting firewall with a corporate CA pushed to every managed device via MDM or group policy."

What is the difference between HTTP and HTTPS?

Asked as: "Diff between HTTP and HTTPS." Give the table, then add what HTTPS does not protect — that closing point is what makes the answer memorable.

The comparison

What TLS adds, in one line each

Confidentiality — nobody on the path can read the content. Integrity — nobody can modify it undetected. Authentication — you're talking to the real server, not an impostor.

The handshake, briefly

Client sends a ClientHello with supported TLS versions, cipher suites and the hostname in SNI. Server replies with its chosen cipher suite and its certificate chain. The client validates the chain up to a trusted root, checks the hostname, validity dates and revocation. Both sides perform an ECDHE key exchange to derive a shared secret neither transmitted, confirm with Finished messages, and from then on everything is symmetrically encrypted — asymmetric crypto is used only to establish the key, because it's far slower. TLS 1.3 completes this in a single round trip.

The point that lands: HTTPS secures data in transit only. It does nothing about SQL injection, XSS, a weak password, a compromised server, or a phishing site — a phishing page can hold a perfectly valid certificate. The padlock means "encrypted to this server", not "this server is trustworthy".

What encryption standards are you familiar with?

Asked as: "Encryption standards." Organise the answer by category rather than reciting names, and be precise that hashing is not encryption.

Symmetric — one shared key, fast, used for bulk data

  • AES — the standard. Block size 128 bits, key sizes 128, 192 or 256. Hardware-accelerated by AES-NI. What matters is the mode: ECB is insecure because identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext (the famous penguin image); CBC needs a random IV and is vulnerable to padding-oracle attacks if done carelessly; CTR turns it into a stream cipher; GCM is the default choice because it provides authenticated encryption — confidentiality and integrity together — but a reused nonce is catastrophic.
  • ChaCha20-Poly1305 — an AEAD stream cipher, faster than AES on hardware without AES-NI, which is why mobile devices favour it.
  • DES and 3DES — deprecated. DES has a 56-bit key, brute-forceable; 3DES is slow and retired.

Asymmetric — a public/private key pair

  • RSA — 2048-bit minimum, 3072 or 4096 preferred. Use OAEP padding for encryption and PSS for signatures; textbook RSA and PKCS#1 v1.5 have known weaknesses.
  • Elliptic curve — ECDSA and Ed25519 for signatures, ECDH/ECDHE for key exchange. Much smaller keys for equivalent strength: a 256-bit ECC key is roughly comparable to RSA-3072, which is why TLS and SSH have moved to it.
  • Diffie-Hellman — lets two parties derive a shared secret over a public channel; the ephemeral variant is what gives TLS forward secrecy.
  • Asymmetric crypto is slow, so it's used to exchange a symmetric key, not to encrypt bulk data. That's hybrid encryption.

Hashing — one-way, not encryption

  • SHA-2 (SHA-256, SHA-512) and SHA-3 are current. MD5 and SHA-1 are broken by collision attacks — never use them for security.
  • HMAC combines a hash with a secret key to prove integrity and authenticity of a message.
  • Hashes are for integrity, fingerprints and signatures. They're irreversible, so you never "decrypt" a hash.

Password storage — a separate category, and a favourite trap

Never encrypt passwords, and never use a plain fast hash like SHA-256 — GPUs compute billions per second. Use a deliberately slow, memory-hard function with a unique random salt per password: Argon2id (current recommendation), bcrypt, scrypt, or PBKDF2 where compliance demands it. Salt defeats rainbow tables; a pepper stored outside the database adds another layer.

Standards and protocols to name

TLS 1.2 and 1.3 for transport; X.509 and PKI for certificates; PKCS standards for key and message formats; JWT with JWS or JWE for tokens; PGP/GPG for files and email; S/MIME; IPsec for VPNs; SSH; the Signal protocol for end-to-end messaging; WPA3 for Wi-Fi. For data at rest: full-disk encryption (BitLocker, LUKS), database TDE, and envelope encryption in cloud KMS where a data key is itself encrypted by a master key held in an HSM. FIPS 140-3 is the validation standard you'll hear in enterprise contexts.

Two things to close with

Key management is the hard part — the algorithm is rarely the weak point. Rotation, storage in a KMS or HSM, access control and secure destruction are what actually fail. And post-quantum is now real: NIST standardised ML-KEM (from Kyber) for key encapsulation and ML-DSA (from Dilithium) for signatures, because a future quantum computer breaks RSA and ECC via Shor's algorithm, while symmetric crypto only needs doubled key sizes. "Harvest now, decrypt later" is why long-lived secrets are being migrated already.

Golden rule: don't roll your own crypto, and don't assemble primitives yourself. Use a vetted library such as libsodium or Tink, and use it in the mode its documentation recommends.

What is the difference between a primary key and a foreign key?

Asked as: "Foreign key primary key." Define both, contrast them in a table, then show a two-table example — having the DDL ready makes this answer complete.

Definitions

A primary key is the column or set of columns that uniquely identifies each row in a table. A foreign key is a column in one table that references the primary key of another, creating the link between them and enforcing referential integrity — you cannot insert a row that points at a parent that doesn't exist.

Primary keyForeign key
PurposeUniquely identifies a rowLinks a row to a row in another table
NULL allowedNo, neverYes — a nullable FK means "no parent yet"
DuplicatesNot allowedAllowed — many students in one department
How many per tableExactly oneAs many as needed
IndexCreated automatically; clustered by default in many enginesAuto-indexed in MySQL InnoDB, but must be created manually in some engines — do it, or joins and cascades get slow
DeletionRow can't be deleted while a child FK references it, unless a cascade rule says otherwiseChild rows can be deleted freely
CompositionCan be composite, but a surrogate single column is usually preferredMust match the referenced key's columns and types

Example

CREATE TABLE departments (
    dept_id    INT PRIMARY KEY,
    dept_name  VARCHAR(50) NOT NULL UNIQUE
);

CREATE TABLE students (
    student_id INT PRIMARY KEY,
    name       VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
    email      VARCHAR(100) UNIQUE,          -- candidate / alternate key
    dept_id    INT,
    CONSTRAINT fk_student_dept
        FOREIGN KEY (dept_id) REFERENCES departments(dept_id)
        ON DELETE SET NULL
        ON UPDATE CASCADE
);

The referential actions — a very common follow-up

  • RESTRICT / NO ACTION — block the delete if children exist. The safe default.
  • CASCADE — delete or update the children too. Powerful and dangerous: one delete can wipe a subtree.
  • SET NULL — orphan the child by nulling the reference; requires a nullable column.
  • SET DEFAULT — point the child at a default parent.

Related key vocabulary

  • Super key — any set of columns that uniquely identifies a row.
  • Candidate key — a minimal super key; a table can have several.
  • Primary key — the candidate key you chose.
  • Alternate key — the candidate keys you didn't choose.
  • Composite key — a key made of more than one column, common in junction tables for many-to-many relationships.
  • Surrogate vs natural key — a meaningless auto-increment id versus real-world data like an email or Aadhaar number. Surrogate keys are preferred because natural values change, and a changing primary key is painful.
  • Unique key vs primary key — unique allows NULLs (one in SQL Server, many in MySQL and Postgres) and you can have several per table.
  • A self-referencing foreign key models hierarchies, such as manager_id referencing employee_id in the same table.

Model a Bisleri water bottle using OOP. What's the superclass and what's the subclass?

Asked as: "Bisleri bottle → sub class super class." They're testing whether you can pick the right abstraction level, and whether you know when not to use inheritance.

The hierarchy

Container (abstract)          // most general: holds something
   └── Bottle (abstract)      // has a cap, a capacity, can be opened and poured
        ├── WaterBottle       // contents are water
        │     ├── PackagedDrinkingWaterBottle   // sealed, branded, has MRP
        │     └── ReusableWaterBottle           // refillable, no MRP
        └── SoftDrinkBottle

So: Bottle is the superclass, WaterBottle is a subclass of it, and a Bisleri bottle is a packaged drinking water bottle. Say the inheritance test out loud as you go: "a water bottle is a bottle, so inheritance is right here."

The code

abstract class Bottle {
    protected double capacityMl;
    protected String  material;
    protected boolean isOpen;

    Bottle(double capacityMl, String material) {
        this.capacityMl = capacityMl;
        this.material   = material;
    }

    void open()  { isOpen = true;  }
    void close() { isOpen = false; }

    abstract void pour(double ml);      // every bottle pours differently
    abstract String getContentType();   // subclass must answer
}

class WaterBottle extends Bottle {
    protected double currentVolumeMl;

    WaterBottle(double capacityMl, String material) {
        super(capacityMl, material);
    }

    @Override String getContentType() { return "Drinking water"; }

    @Override void pour(double ml) {
        if (!isOpen) throw new IllegalStateException("Open the bottle first");
        if (ml > currentVolumeMl) throw new IllegalArgumentException("Not enough water");
        currentVolumeMl -= ml;
    }
}

class PackagedWaterBottle extends WaterBottle implements Recyclable, Sealable {
    private final String brand;        // "Bisleri"
    private final double mrp;
    private final String batchNumber;
    private boolean sealIntact = true;

    PackagedWaterBottle(String brand, double capacityMl, double mrp, String batch) {
        super(capacityMl, "PET plastic");
        this.brand = brand;
        this.mrp = mrp;
        this.batchNumber = batch;
        this.currentVolumeMl = capacityMl;
    }

    @Override public void breakSeal() { sealIntact = false; open(); }
    @Override public String recycleCode() { return "PET-1"; }
}

The insight that scores the marks

Bisleri is a brand, not a class. If I created a BisleriBottle class, I'd need KinleyBottle, AquafinaBottle and a new class for every brand and every size — a class explosion for data that never changes behaviour. Brand and capacity are attributes, so a Bisleri 1-litre bottle is an instance: new PackagedWaterBottle("Bisleri", 1000, 20.0, "B2419"). I'd only subclass if the behaviour genuinely differed.

Everything else they can probe from here

  • Abstraction — Bottle is abstract because a generic "bottle" can't exist on its own, and it has abstract methods every subclass must implement.
  • Encapsulation — currentVolumeMl is private or protected with controlled access, so nobody can set a volume above capacity.
  • Inheritance — shared state and behaviour live once, in the parent.
  • Polymorphism — Bottle b = new PackagedWaterBottle(...); b.pour(200); runs the subclass implementation, decided at runtime.
  • Interfaces for capabilities — Recyclable, Sealable, Refillable. A capability that cuts across unrelated classes belongs in an interface, not the hierarchy. A cardboard box is recyclable but is not a bottle.
  • Composition over inheritance — a bottle has a Cap and a Label. Those are separate objects, not parent classes, because it's a has-a relationship.
  • Liskov substitution — anywhere the code expects a Bottle, a PackagedWaterBottle must work without surprises. If a subclass has to throw "unsupported operation" on an inherited method, the hierarchy is wrong.

The simple answer

SELECT s.dept_id
FROM   marks m
JOIN   students s ON s.student_id = m.student_id
WHERE  m.subject = 'Maths'
ORDER  BY m.marks DESC
LIMIT  1;

The version that handles ties

SELECT DISTINCT s.dept_id
FROM   marks m
JOIN   students s ON s.student_id = m.student_id
WHERE  m.subject = 'Maths'
  AND  m.marks = (SELECT MAX(marks) FROM marks WHERE subject = 'Maths');

The window-function version — show this if they want more

SELECT dept_id, name, marks
FROM (
    SELECT s.dept_id,
           s.name,
           m.marks,
           RANK() OVER (ORDER BY m.marks DESC) AS rnk
    FROM   marks m
    JOIN   students s ON s.student_id = m.student_id
    WHERE  m.subject = 'Maths'
) ranked
WHERE rnk = 1;

Explain the choice of ranking function, because it's a guaranteed follow-up: RANK() gives tied rows the same rank and skips the next value (1,1,3), DENSE_RANK() doesn't skip (1,1,2), and ROW_NUMBER() forces a unique ordering (1,2,3) which would silently drop a tie.

If the marks table is wide instead

FROM   scores sc
JOIN   students s ON s.student_id = sc.student_id

Explain inheritance, multiple inheritance, interfaces and abstract methods

Asked as: "OOPS inheritance, multiple inheritance, interfaces, abstract methods." Four linked concepts — answer them as one connected story, ending with when you'd choose an interface over an abstract class.

Inheritance

A mechanism where a child class acquires the fields and methods of a parent class, so shared behaviour is written once and specialised where needed. It models an is-a relationship — a Car is a Vehicle. In Java, class Car extends Vehicle, and super() calls the parent constructor, which always runs before the child's body.

Types, with one line each:

  • Single — one parent, one child.
  • Multilevel — a chain: Vehicle → Car → SportsCar.
  • Hierarchical — one parent, many children.
  • Multiple — one child, several parents. Not supported for classes in Java.
  • Hybrid — a combination of the above.

Multiple inheritance and the diamond problem

If class D inherits from both B and C, and both inherit from A and override the same method, then when you call that method on D the compiler cannot decide which implementation to use. The inheritance graph is diamond-shaped, hence the name. There's also ambiguity over how many copies of A's state D holds.

How different languages resolve it:

  • Java forbids multiple inheritance of classes entirely, but allows a class to implement many interfaces — interfaces traditionally carried no state or implementation, so there was nothing to be ambiguous about. Since Java 8 interfaces can have default methods, so the problem partly returned; Java handles it by making the conflict a compile error that you must resolve explicitly with InterfaceName.super.method().
  • C++ allows it, and you resolve the duplicated base with virtual inheritance so only one copy of A exists.
  • Python allows it and resolves order deterministically using the MRO, computed by C3 linearisation, which you can inspect with ClassName.__mro__.

Abstract classes and abstract methods

An abstract method is declared with a signature but no body. Any class containing one must itself be declared abstract, and an abstract class cannot be instantiated — you can only instantiate a concrete subclass. The first concrete subclass must implement every inherited abstract method. Abstract classes can still have constructors (called via super()), fields, and fully implemented concrete methods — that combination of shared state plus enforced contract is exactly what they're for.

Interfaces

A contract: a set of method signatures a class promises to provide. It describes a capability rather than an identity — Comparable, Serializable, Runnable. Methods are implicitly public abstract and fields implicitly public static final. A class can implement any number of interfaces, and interfaces can extend other interfaces. Since Java 8 they may also have default and static methods, and since Java 9, private methods — added so libraries could extend interfaces without breaking every existing implementor.

Rule of thumb: if you want to share implementation, use an abstract class. If you want to define a contract that unrelated classes can satisfy, use an interface. In modern code the interface is usually the default, because it keeps things loosely coupled and testable — and a class can only spend its one extends slot once.

Adjacent things they will probe

  • Overriding vs overloading. Overriding: a subclass redefines an inherited method with the same signature — runtime polymorphism, resolved by dynamic dispatch through the virtual method table. Overloading: same method name, different parameter list in the same class — compile-time polymorphism, resolved by the compiler from the argument types.
  • Rules for overriding — same name and parameters, return type must be the same or a subtype (covariant), access cannot be more restrictive, and you can't override final, static or private methods. A static method redefined in a subclass is hiding, not overriding.
  • Why composition is often better — inheritance couples the child to the parent's implementation, so a parent change breaks children. "Favour composition over inheritance."
  • Interfaces enable dependency inversion — code against the interface, inject the implementation, and testing with a mock becomes trivial.

Cybersecurity — the broad round

Asked as just "cyber sec." When the prompt is this open, they'll pick something from a standard set. Here is that set, compressed — question 6 covers vulnerability vs threat vs attack, question 7 covers DDoS, question 8 covers finding vulnerabilities and SQL injection, so this is everything else.

Foundations

  • CIA triad — Confidentiality (only the right people can read it), Integrity (nobody altered it), Availability (it's there when needed). Add non-repudiation and authenticity for the fuller picture.
  • Authentication vs authorisation — authentication proves who you are; authorisation decides what you may do. Login vs permissions. Accounting is the audit trail of what you did.
  • Encoding vs hashing vs encryption — encoding (Base64) is for data format and is trivially reversible, it is not security. Hashing is one-way. Encryption is two-way with a key. Candidates are routinely rejected for confusing these.
  • Zero trust — never trust based on network location; authenticate and authorise every request, verify device posture, and grant the least privilege for the shortest time.

The web attacks you must be able to explain

  • XSS — attacker-supplied script executes in another user's browser. Stored (saved in the database, served to everyone), reflected (bounced back from a URL parameter), DOM-based (client-side JavaScript writes untrusted input into the page). Impact: session theft, actions as the victim. Fix: context-aware output encoding, a framework that escapes by default, Content-Security-Policy, HttpOnly cookies, and validating input on the way in.
  • CSRF — the attacker's page makes the victim's browser send an authenticated request to your site, exploiting the fact that cookies are attached automatically. Fix: anti-CSRF tokens, SameSite cookies, and requiring state-changing actions to use POST with an origin check. The difference from XSS: XSS abuses the site's trust in user input, CSRF abuses the site's trust in the user's browser.
  • IDOR / broken object-level authorisation — changing /api/invoice/1042 to 1043 and seeing someone else's data. Fix: check ownership on every request server-side; unguessable ids are not a fix.
  • SSRF — you make the server fetch a URL the attacker controls, reaching internal services or cloud metadata endpoints. Fix: allow-list destinations, block internal address ranges.
  • Broken authentication — weak passwords, no lockout, predictable session ids, sessions that never expire, tokens in URLs.
  • Security misconfiguration — default credentials, directory listing enabled, debug mode in production, public cloud storage buckets.
  • Vulnerable dependencies — the most common real-world breach path, and the easiest to fix with automated scanning.

Network and defensive side

  • Firewall — packet filtering (headers only), stateful inspection (tracks connections), next-generation (application-aware). A WAF operates at layer 7 specifically for HTTP.
  • IDS vs IPS — an IDS detects and alerts; an IPS sits inline and blocks. Detection is either signature-based (known patterns, misses novel attacks) or anomaly-based (baseline deviation, more false positives).
  • VPN — an encrypted tunnel over an untrusted network. SIEM — central log aggregation and correlation for the SOC.
  • Defence in depth — layered controls so one failure doesn't become a breach.

Incident response — the NIST phases

Preparation → Detection and analysis → Containment (short-term isolation, then long-term) → Eradication (remove the foothold, patch the root cause) → Recovery (restore and monitor) → Lessons learned. Say that you preserve evidence before wiping anything, and that the post-mortem should be blameless and produce concrete action items.

Testing and teams

Penetration testing phases: planning and scoping, reconnaissance, scanning and enumeration, exploitation, post-exploitation and privilege escalation, reporting, retest. Black box (no knowledge), white box (full access), grey box (partial). Red team attacks, blue team defends, purple team is the two working together. DevSecOps means shifting these checks left into the CI pipeline rather than testing at the end.

What is transfer learning?

Asked as: "Transfer learning." Define it, explain the two ways of doing it, then give the practical details — learning rates, frozen layers, when it fails. Details are what prove you've actually trained something.

Definition

Transfer learning reuses a model trained on a large source task as the starting point for a different but related target task, so the representations it already learned are transferred instead of being learned again from scratch. A network trained on ImageNet has already learned edges, textures, shapes and object parts in its early and middle layers — those features are useful for almost any vision task, so only the task-specific part needs to be relearned.

Why it's used

  • Small datasets. With 2,000 labelled images you cannot train a deep network from scratch without overfitting, but you can fine-tune one.
  • Compute and time. Minutes on one GPU instead of days on a cluster.
  • Better accuracy and faster convergence, because you start from a good region of the loss surface rather than random weights.

The two approaches

Feature extractionFine-tuning
What you doFreeze the pretrained backbone, replace the final classifier head, train only the headUnfreeze some or all layers and continue training them on the new data
Trainable paramsVery fewMany
Use whenTarget dataset is small and similar to the sourceTarget dataset is larger, or the domain differs (e.g. medical scans vs ImageNet)
RiskUnderfitting if the domain gap is largeOverfitting and catastrophic forgetting

The usual recipe is both, in order: freeze and train the head until it stabilises, then unfreeze the top blocks and continue at a much lower learning rate. Gradual unfreezing and discriminative learning rates — smaller rates for earlier layers, larger for later ones — work well, because early layers hold generic features you don't want to disturb.

Practical details worth naming

  • Learning rate for fine-tuning should be an order of magnitude or two below what you'd use for training from scratch — around 1e-4 to 1e-5. Too high and you destroy the pretrained weights in the first few batches.
  • Match the preprocessing exactly — the same input size and the same normalisation statistics the pretrained model was trained with. A frequent silent bug.
  • Batch normalisation layers are often kept frozen in inference mode when the new batch size is small, since updating their running statistics on a tiny batch hurts.
  • Regularise — augmentation, dropout, early stopping — because the model has far more capacity than your dataset justifies.
  • Catastrophic forgetting — fine-tuning too aggressively erases the general knowledge you wanted to keep.
  • Negative transfer — when the source and target domains are too dissimilar, transfer can perform worse than training from scratch. Audio spectrograms from ImageNet weights work surprisingly well; genuinely unrelated signal domains don't.

Where it shows up

  • Vision: take ResNet, EfficientNet or a Vision Transformer pretrained on ImageNet, replace the final fully connected layer with one of N outputs for your classes, and fine-tune.
  • NLP: this is now the default paradigm. BERT-style models are pretrained on masked language modelling, then fine-tuned for classification, NER or question answering with a small task head.
  • Large language models: pretraining, then instruction tuning, then preference tuning. Because full fine-tuning of a huge model is expensive, PEFT methods are used — LoRA trains small low-rank adapter matrices while the base weights stay frozen, and QLoRA does this on a quantised base model, cutting memory enormously with little quality loss.
  • Related ideas: domain adaptation (same task, different data distribution) and multi-task learning (shared representation across several tasks at once).
For an LLM task, mention the ladder: try prompting first, then few-shot examples, then RAG if the gap is missing knowledge, and fine-tune only when the gap is behaviour, format or tone. Reaching for fine-tuning first is the common mistake.

  1. The project, in two sentences. What it does, who uses it, and its rough scale. "It's an open-source library for X, used by Y kind of developer, around Z thousand stars, written in [language]."
  2. Why you picked it. A real reason beats a noble one: "I was using it in my own project and hit a bug" is the strongest possible answer, because it explains how you understood the codebase.
  3. The contribution, specifically. The issue, what was broken or missing, how you reproduced it, what the root cause turned out to be, what you changed, and what tests you added.
  4. The process. Read CONTRIBUTING.md, forked, branched, wrote the fix and a regression test, ran the linter and test suite locally, opened a PR referencing the issue, went through N rounds of review, rebased on main, and it was merged.
  5. Impact and learning. What changed for users, and one honest thing you learned.

Don't invent one. Say: "I haven't had a PR merged yet. I've been reading through the issues on [project] labelled good-first-issue, and I've got the development environment running locally.   • Fork vs clone — a fork is your own server-side copy of the repository; a clone is a local copy. You fork because you don't have push access to the original.

  • Merge conflict — the same lines changed in both branches; git marks them and you choose the correct combined result, then continue the rebase or merge.

  • Project conventions — CONTRIBUTING.md, code of conduct, a CLA or DCO sign-off, conventional commit messages, issue templates, and the CI that must pass before review.
  • Licences — know your project's: MIT and Apache 2.0 are permissive, GPL is copyleft and requires derivative works to be open too.

01Bangalore traffic and city systems

How would you estimate the number of cars on Bangalore roads at 9 a.m.?

I would use a Fermi estimate and state every assumption. Start with the metropolitan population, estimate households, cars per household, and the share of cars in use during the morning peak. Cross-check the result from the road side: major corridors times lanes times vehicles per lane per hour, plus local roads. Give a range, then say which assumption you would validate first with registration and traffic-count data.

How would you design fair congestion pricing?

Charge vehicles that enter the most congested zone during peak hours, using number-plate cameras and a daily cap. Vary the price by time and vehicle type, publish the rule clearly, and begin with a pilot. Make it fair by exempting emergency and disability vehicles, offering income-based credits, and spending the revenue on frequent buses and last-mile links. Judge it by travel time, traffic volume, bus ridership and impact by income group.

How would you improve metro ridership?

First identify whether the main loss occurs before the station, during interchange, or after the last stop. Improve feeder buses, walking routes, cycle parking, frequency and interchange signs; integrate fares so one pass covers metro and bus; and coordinate service with office peaks. Run corridor pilots and measure entries, repeat riders, door-to-door time and the percentage of trips completed without a private vehicle.

How would you solve last-mile connectivity?

Map the one-to-three-kilometre catchment around busy stations and segment users by need. Provide timed feeder buses on dense routes, regulated shared autos on medium-density routes, and safe walking and cycling infrastructure for short trips. A common app and integrated fare reduce friction. Track wait time, transfer time, cost per passenger and the share of riders reaching the station without a private car.

How would you reduce road accidents in a city?

Use a safe-system approach: people will make mistakes, so roads and speeds must keep those mistakes survivable. Build a crash map, fix the worst junctions, separate pedestrians and two-wheelers, improve lighting and crossings, and enforce helmets, seat belts, drink-driving and speeding. Pair enforcement with faster trauma response. Measure deaths and serious injuries per trip, not just the raw number of reported crashes.

How would data decide where to build the next flyover?

I would not begin by assuming a flyover is the answer. Rank junctions by person-delay, queue length, crash history, bus movement and projected demand, then model alternatives such as signal changes, turn restrictions and bus priority. Build only if simulation and a cost-benefit study show durable network-wide improvement; a flyover that moves a bottleneck 500 metres downstream has failed.

How would you design an accurate commute-time app?

Combine a road graph with live GPS speeds, historical speed by road segment and time, weather, incidents, holidays and planned closures. Predict segment travel times, run a shortest-time route algorithm, and update the estimate as new observations arrive. Train and evaluate on trips separated by time to avoid leakage. Report an interval such as 35–42 minutes and track median absolute error plus calibration of that interval.

How would you solve garbage collection or water supply?

For garbage, map generation and missed-pickup hotspots, separate wet and dry waste at source, optimise vehicle routes, track collections with GPS, and pay contractors against verified service and processing outcomes. For water, meter bulk flows, divide the network into pressure zones, detect night-flow leaks, repair high-loss segments and publish supply schedules. In both cases, pilot one ward and measure service reliability, cost and citizen complaints.

How would you spend ₹100 crore in one year?

I would state that the exact split follows a baseline study, then propose a portfolio: roughly ₹40 crore for high-frequency electric or low-emission feeder buses and depots, ₹25 crore for bus-priority and junction fixes, ₹15 crore for safe footpaths and crossings near transit, ₹10 crore for adaptive signals and traffic data, and ₹10 crore for incident-clearance teams and evaluation. Reserve payments for milestones and choose corridors where work can finish within a year.

How would you measure whether the intervention worked?

Define the primary outcome before launch—for example median peak travel time or bus person-throughput—and record several weeks of baseline data. Compare the treated corridor with a similar untreated corridor using before-and-after measurements, while controlling for weather, holidays and construction. Also track safety, emissions, mode share and equity so an apparent speed gain has not simply displaced congestion or harmed pedestrians.

02BookMyShow chatbot

How do you handle a user changing details mid-booking?

Store booking details as a typed state object rather than only in chat text. Detect which slots changed, invalidate dependent values—for example a new date invalidates the selected show and seats—and preserve independent values such as city. Re-query availability, show the revised summary, and require confirmation before holding seats or charging. Keep an event log so retries and support staff can reconstruct the state.

How do you stop hallucinated refund policies?

Make the policy service or approved policy documents the only source of refund rules. Retrieve by booking type, theatre, date and offer, require a citation or structured tool result, and instruct the model to say it cannot confirm when evidence is missing. Validate amounts in code, not in the model, and regression-test difficult examples. A refund action itself remains a deterministic backend operation with explicit user confirmation.

How do you evaluate a chatbot?

Measure task completion and containment, intent or tool-selection accuracy, slot accuracy, turns to completion, fallback and handoff rates, latency, cost, user satisfaction and repeat contact. For booking, add conversion, payment failure, incorrect-booking and cancellation rates. Use a fixed offline test set for every release, then A/B test with guardrail metrics; a fluent answer is not successful if the booking state is wrong.

How would you handle Hinglish and regional languages?

Detect language per turn but keep the user’s selected language as a preference. Use multilingual intent and entity models or an LLM that handles code-switching, normalise transliterated names, and maintain aliases for films, theatres and locations. Test with native speakers across scripts, dialects and mixed sentences. Keep critical confirmations—movie, date, seats and amount—structured and display them unambiguously before payment.

How do you manage conversation state at scale?

Keep a compact server-side state keyed by conversation ID: current intent, filled slots, pending action, version and expiry. Store it in a durable low-latency database, use optimistic locking to prevent concurrent updates, and make backend operations idempotent. Summarise old conversational text instead of sending the full history. Encrypt sensitive fields, limit retention and never place payment credentials in model context or logs.

How would you reduce LLM bot cost and latency?

Route greetings, FAQs and simple classifications to rules or a small model; use the larger model only for ambiguous turns. Reduce prompt and retrieved context, cache safe repeated answers, stream responses, run independent calls in parallel and avoid calling the model after deterministic tool results can render a template. Track cost and p95 latency by intent so optimisation targets the expensive path without lowering completion quality.

When would you choose a rule-based bot?

Choose it when intents are few, the dialogue is predictable, latency and cost must be minimal, or every response must be auditable—for example OTP verification or a regulated payment confirmation. Rules are also a good fallback during model outages. I would still measure fallback frequency: if paraphrases and changing requirements create too many unhandled branches, use an LLM for understanding while retaining the deterministic transaction flow.

How should the bot handle payment failures and partial bookings?

Give every payment attempt and booking an idempotency key. Hold seats for a short expiry, record payment and booking as separate state transitions, and confirm only after the payment provider and booking service agree. If money is captured but booking fails, trigger an automatic reversal or reconciliation job and show an honest pending status. Never ask the user to pay again until the first attempt is resolved.

Design seat locking so two users cannot book the same seat.

Create a short-lived hold with an atomic database operation such as an update conditioned on the seat still being AVAILABLE. A unique constraint on show ID plus seat ID is the final defence. Store the hold owner and expiry, release expired holds through a worker, and use the same idempotency key on retries. Payment converts the valid hold to BOOKED in a transaction; a stale client must recheck availability.

How would you add voice input, and what breaks?

Add speech-to-text before NLU and text-to-speech after the response, with streaming and interruption support. New failure modes include noisy audio, accents, names being transcribed incorrectly, high latency and privacy concerns. Show or repeat recognised movie, theatre, date, seats and price before any irreversible step, support touch correction, and measure word error rate as well as end-to-end task completion.

03RAG pipelines

What chunk size would you pick, and how does overlap help?

I would start around 300–600 tokens, split on headings and paragraphs, then tune using retrieval evaluation. Small chunks are precise but may lose context; large chunks preserve context but add noise and consume the prompt. A modest overlap keeps facts spanning a boundary in at least one chunk, but excessive overlap creates duplicates. Parent-child retrieval often works better: retrieve a small passage and supply its larger section.

What is a vector database and approximate nearest-neighbour search?

A vector database stores embeddings plus metadata and finds items whose vectors are close to a query vector. Exact comparison with every vector is costly, so approximate indexes such as HNSW or IVF search a much smaller candidate set. They trade a little recall for large speed and memory gains. Filters, index parameters and the chosen distance metric must be evaluated on the actual corpus.

What is cosine similarity?

Cosine similarity is the dot product divided by the two vector lengths, so it measures the angle between vectors rather than their magnitude. Values closer to 1 indicate similar direction. It is common for text embeddings because semantic direction is often more useful than vector length; if embeddings are normalised to unit length, cosine ranking is equivalent to dot-product ranking.

What is hybrid search, and why keep BM25?

Hybrid search combines dense vector retrieval with lexical retrieval such as BM25, then fuses or reranks their results. Vectors handle paraphrases and concepts; BM25 is strong for exact names, IDs, error codes and rare terms that embeddings may blur. Use reciprocal-rank fusion as a simple baseline and test weights by query type. The combination usually gives more reliable recall across a mixed enterprise corpus.

What is reranking and where does a cross-encoder fit?

Initial retrieval cheaply returns perhaps 30–100 candidates. A reranker then scores each candidate more carefully and keeps the best few for the prompt. A cross-encoder reads the query and passage together, so it captures fine interactions better than comparing separate embeddings, but it is slower. Use it after retrieval, not across the whole corpus, and measure whether the added latency improves answer accuracy.

How do you evaluate RAG without labelled data?

Create a small seed set from real queries, document headings and support logs, then have domain experts judge retrieved passages and answers. Synthetic question generation can expand coverage, but sample and verify it because it can favour the generator. Track retrieval relevance, faithfulness to context, answer usefulness and citation correctness separately. Production signals such as reformulation and escalation help find cases to add to the test set.

How do you stop RAG hallucination?

You reduce it rather than promise elimination. Improve retrieval, pass only relevant evidence, instruct the model to answer from that evidence, require sentence-level citations, and allow “I don’t have enough information.” Verify quoted facts or structured fields against the retrieved text, set confidence thresholds and send high-impact answers to review. Evaluate faithfulness and citation support, not just whether the prose sounds plausible.

How do you handle tables, images and scanned PDFs?

Use layout-aware parsing so headings, reading order and table cells survive extraction. Run OCR on scanned pages and store page coordinates for citations. Convert simple tables to structured text or rows with repeated headers; for complex figures, store the image and a verified caption or use a multimodal model at query time. Keep a link to the original page because extraction errors are inevitable.

What is Graph RAG, and when does it beat vector RAG?

Graph RAG extracts entities and relationships into a graph, then retrieves connected facts or community summaries. It helps with multi-hop questions such as how two organisations, people and events are related, or with corpus-wide themes that no single chunk contains. It costs more to build and maintain, and extraction errors propagate. For ordinary factual lookup, strong hybrid vector search is usually simpler and better.

How do you keep an index fresh and handle deletions?

Give each source a stable document ID, content hash, version and access-control metadata. A change-data or scheduled pipeline reprocesses only changed documents and upserts their chunks. On deletion, tombstone the document immediately so retrieval filters it, then remove its vectors and caches asynchronously. Use blue-green indexes for large rebuilds, audit source-to-chunk counts and test that permission changes reach the index quickly.

04AI agents

Explain the ReAct pattern with an example.

ReAct alternates reasoning about the next step with an action through a tool, then uses the observation to decide what follows. For “Will it rain during my Delhi flight?”, an agent can look up the itinerary, call a weather service for the arrival window, observe the forecast and answer. Production systems should keep hidden reasoning private and log concise action traces, tool inputs and results for debugging.

How does tool calling work under the hood?

The application sends the model tool names, descriptions and argument schemas. The model returns a structured request such as a tool name plus JSON arguments; application code validates permissions and arguments, executes the real function, and sends the result back to the model. The model does not directly run code. The loop continues until it returns a final response or reaches a step, time or cost limit.

How would you give an agent memory across sessions?

Separate memory into durable facts, episodic summaries and retrievable history. Save only useful, consented facts with source, timestamp, owner and expiry; retrieve a small relevant subset for each task. Do not treat model-written summaries as unquestionable truth—let users inspect and correct them. Encrypt sensitive data, apply tenant access controls and define deletion and retention policies. The current task state belongs in a normal database.

How do you stop infinite loops or budget burn?

Set hard limits on steps, wall-clock time, tokens, tool calls and spend. Detect repeated tool-and-argument pairs, require each step to change state, and stop after consecutive errors. Use timeouts and circuit breakers around tools, checkpoint useful progress and return a clear partial result. High-cost branches or limit increases require human approval. Monitor per-task cost and loop signatures in production.

What is prompt injection, and how do you defend an agent?

Prompt injection is untrusted text that tries to override the agent’s instructions or misuse its tools. Treat retrieved pages and messages as data, isolate them from system rules, allowlist tools and destinations, validate every action in code, and grant short-lived least-privilege credentials. Require confirmation for consequential actions and never expose secrets to the model. Content filters help, but permission boundaries are the real control.

How would you test and evaluate an agent?

Build scenario tests with expected outcomes, allowed tools and forbidden side effects. Score task success, tool-selection and argument accuracy, number of steps, recovery from tool errors, latency and cost. Add adversarial cases for injection, bad data and permission boundaries. Run tools in a sandbox or simulator, replay fixed traces on every change, and review sampled production runs because end-to-end success can hide unsafe intermediate actions.

When is multi-agent better or worse?

Multiple agents help when work divides into genuinely independent specialties, can run in parallel, and has clear handoff contracts—for example research, code analysis and verification. They hurt when tasks share lots of context or need tight sequential decisions: coordination adds tokens, latency, duplicated work and new failure modes. Start with one agent plus tools; split only after evaluation shows a specific bottleneck that separation improves.

How would you design an agent allowed to spend money?

Give it a dedicated account with per-transaction, daily and category limits. The model may propose a purchase, but deterministic code validates vendor, amount, policy, budget and duplicate risk. Show the user the exact item, total and cancellation terms, then require explicit approval or a second approver above a threshold. Use idempotency keys, signed receipts, an immutable audit log and an immediate kill switch.

What is the Model Context Protocol?

MCP is an open protocol that standardises how AI applications connect to external capabilities. A server can expose tools, resources and reusable prompts; a client discovers and invokes them through a common contract instead of a custom integration for every data source. It improves portability and discovery, but does not remove the need for authentication, consent, input validation or least-privilege tool design.

Build an agent for X—what tools and prohibitions would it have?

I would first define the outcome, users, data and cost of a wrong action. Give it the minimum read tools needed to investigate, narrow write tools with typed schemas, and a status or rollback tool. I would forbid direct database, shell, secret and unrestricted network access. Deletion, payment, publication and messages to people need validation and approval. Add limits, audit logs, dry-run mode and outcome-based tests.

05AI workflows

When does prompt chaining beat one large prompt?

Use a chain when each stage has a different goal or can be checked independently. For a research brief: extract claims and sources, verify support, create an outline, then draft from the approved outline. Each output has a schema and quality check. This costs more calls, but failures are visible and retryable; one large prompt often mixes research, judgement and writing so an error is hard to locate.

How would you route between cheap and expensive models?

Handle deterministic cases with code, then use rules or a small classifier based on intent, length, language, required tools and risk. Send simple summarisation and extraction to the cheaper model; send ambiguous, long-context or high-value tasks to the stronger one. Escalate when confidence is low or validation fails. Evaluate routing against quality, latency and cost, and keep a small random sample on both models to detect drift.

How do you get reliably parseable structured output?

Define a strict JSON schema with required fields, types, enums and bounds, and use the provider’s schema-constrained output or tool-calling mode. Validate server-side and reject unknown fields. If validation fails, retry once with the error and original input, then fall back safely. Do not extract JSON from decorative prose with regex, and still validate business rules such as dates, permissions and totals after syntax passes.

How do retries work with a non-deterministic LLM?

Retry transient transport errors with exponential backoff and jitter, but retry quality failures only after giving the model the validation error or changing the strategy. Cap attempts and record each one. Make surrounding tool calls idempotent so a repeated model turn cannot send two emails or create two payments. Reuse a request ID, and return a clear failure or human handoff instead of looping.

How would you build an LLM evaluation suite?

Collect representative and difficult real examples, remove sensitive data, and define task-specific expected facts, schemas or rubrics. Include normal, edge, adversarial and refusal cases. Prefer deterministic checks where possible; use blinded human review for usefulness and pairwise preference, with a calibrated model judge only as a scalable supplement. Track quality, safety, latency and cost by slice, and block releases on material regressions.

How do you version and roll back prompts?

Store prompts, schemas, model settings, retrieval configuration and tool definitions as versioned code. Every request logs the exact version. Run offline evaluation and a small canary before increasing traffic, keep the previous compatible version deployable, and use a feature flag for instant rollback. Database or output-schema changes need backward compatibility so rolling back the prompt does not break downstream consumers.

How would you halve LLM pipeline cost?

Measure token and call cost by stage first. Typical wins are removing duplicated context, retrieving fewer better passages, caching stable results, batching embeddings, routing easy work to smaller models and replacing model calls with templates or code. Set output limits and stop failed branches early. Re-run the evaluation suite after each change; a 50% saving that lowers task completion creates a more expensive product.

Where should human review enter an automated pipeline?

Place review where error impact is high and correction is still cheap: before publishing, payment, denial of service, hiring rejection, legal or medical advice, or changes to production. Route low-confidence, policy-flagged and novel cases rather than reviewing everything. Give reviewers evidence, model output and a clear decision interface. Record corrections to improve evaluation, while checking that automation bias does not turn review into rubber-stamping.

What breaks when an LLM prototype reaches production?

Real inputs are longer, multilingual, malicious and incomplete; dependencies fail; latency and cost vary; prompts and models drift; and users expect consistency, privacy and support. Production therefore needs schemas, timeouts, retries, idempotency, access control, observability, evaluation, fallbacks and incident procedures. The largest gap is usually not model intelligence but ordinary engineering around unpredictable output and external side effects.

Design a resume-screening pipeline and name its risks.

Parse resumes into a fixed schema, extract job-relevant evidence, compare it with explicit criteria, and produce an evidence-backed summary for a trained reviewer. Remove protected attributes where lawful, test performance across groups, support accommodations and appeals, and never let the model invent missing experience. Risks include historical bias, proxy discrimination, parsing errors, privacy leakage and opaque rejection. Keep a human accountable for the hiring decision.

06Security foundations

Explain the CIA triad with an example attack on each pillar.

Confidentiality means only authorised people see data; a stolen customer database violates it. Integrity means data remains accurate and authorised; changing bank balances violates it. Availability means systems and data are usable when needed; a DDoS attack violates it. Controls overlap: encryption supports confidentiality, signatures and audit logs support integrity, and redundancy plus tested recovery support availability.

Risk assessment vs vulnerability assessment?

A vulnerability assessment finds and classifies weaknesses in assets, usually through scanning and review. A risk assessment asks what can happen to the business by combining assets, threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood and impact, including nontechnical risks. A critical-looking vulnerability on an isolated test server may be lower risk than a moderate access-control flaw in a payment system. Remediation priority should follow risk, with urgent exceptions for active exploitation.

What is a zero-day, and how do you defend without a patch?

A zero-day is a vulnerability unknown to, or not yet fixed by, the vendor when attackers can exploit it. Reduce exposure with asset inventory, least privilege, segmentation, application allowlisting, EDR, strong authentication and rapid detection. Apply temporary vendor mitigations, disable the affected feature or block exploit patterns at the edge. Hunt for indicators, preserve evidence, patch as soon as a tested fix exists, and assume prevention may fail.

What are the OWASP Top 10? Explain three.

The OWASP Top 10 is an awareness list of major web-application risk categories; the current edition is 2025. Three examples are broken access control, where users reach data or actions they do not own; security misconfiguration, such as exposed admin interfaces or unsafe defaults; and injection, where untrusted input changes a command or query. Prevent them with server-side authorisation, hardened configuration and parameterised APIs.

What is defence in depth?

Defence in depth uses independent layers so one failed control does not expose the asset. For an internet application: a CDN and WAF reduce hostile traffic, patched services run in segmented networks, strong identity and least privilege limit access, parameterised queries protect the database, encryption protects stored data, and central logs plus alerts detect abuse. Backups and response plans limit damage if every preventive layer is bypassed.

What is least privilege?

Least privilege gives a user, service or process only the permissions needed, for only as long as needed. A reporting service should have read access to selected views, not database-admin rights; a deployment job can receive short-lived credentials for one environment. Apply role-based access, separate duties, remove stale rights and review high-risk grants. This limits both accidental damage and what an attacker gains from one compromised identity.

Explain STRIDE by threat-modelling a login.

Draw the user, browser, API, identity service and database with their trust boundaries. Then apply STRIDE: spoofing through stolen credentials; tampering with requests; repudiation without audit logs; information disclosure through verbose errors; denial of service through login floods; and elevation of privilege through broken role checks. Add MFA, TLS, rate limits, generic errors, protected audit logs and server-side authorisation, then prioritise by likelihood and impact.

What is a supply-chain attack?

It compromises a trusted supplier, dependency, build system or update channel to reach downstream users. SolarWinds is a well-known example: malicious code entered signed Orion updates. Reduce risk with locked and reviewed dependencies, software bills of materials, signature and provenance verification, isolated builds, least-privilege CI credentials, reproducible artefacts where practical and monitoring after deployment. Supplier trust should be verified continuously rather than assumed.

Authentication, authorisation and accounting?

Authentication proves who the subject is, such as password plus MFA. Authorisation decides what that authenticated subject may do, such as whether an employee can approve an invoice. Accounting records what happened—login time, action, resource and result—for audit and investigation. The order is authenticate, authorise every request server-side, then log security-relevant events without leaking passwords, tokens or unnecessary personal data.

What is CVSS, and how do you prioritise 200 findings?

CVSS standardises technical vulnerability severity from characteristics such as attack vector, complexity, privileges and impact. It is an input, not the queue by itself. Combine it with internet exposure, asset criticality, sensitive data, known exploitation, reachability and available compensating controls. Fix actively exploited and exposed paths first, group common root causes, assign owners and deadlines, verify remediation, and formally accept or document residual risk.

07DDoS

DoS vs DDoS?

A denial-of-service attack exhausts a service from one or a small number of sources. A distributed denial-of-service attack uses many machines or reflectors, so blocking one address does not stop it and the total capacity can be much larger. Both target availability. DDoS defence therefore depends on upstream capacity, traffic distribution and distinguishing legitimate clients, not just a firewall beside the application.

Explain a SYN flood.

In a normal TCP handshake the client sends SYN, the server replies SYN-ACK, and the client completes with ACK. A SYN flood sends many SYNs—often with spoofed sources—and does not finish the handshake, consuming the server’s half-open connection queue. Defences include SYN cookies, shorter queues and timeouts, per-source limits, upstream filtering and a DDoS service that absorbs or validates traffic before it reaches the origin.

What is DNS amplification, and how do you prevent reflection?

An attacker sends small DNS queries with the victim’s spoofed source address to open resolvers; the much larger replies hit the victim. Prevent your infrastructure becoming a reflector by disabling open recursion, allowing recursion only for authorised clients, applying response-rate limiting and filtering spoofed traffic at network edges. Victims need upstream scrubbing, anycast capacity and coordination with providers because the link may saturate before local controls run.

DDoS or organic traffic spike?

Compare the event with known campaigns and historical traffic by geography, network, user agent, URL, session behaviour and conversion. Organic users usually load a realistic sequence of pages, accept cookies and create business activity; attacks often show repeated expensive requests, unusual protocol ratios, poor session continuity or concentrated networks. Avoid one-rule blocking: use layered challenges and rate limits while product and operations confirm whether a launch or news event explains demand.

What is a botnet?

A botnet is a group of compromised devices controlled through command-and-control infrastructure. Devices are recruited through weak default passwords, unpatched services, malicious downloads or stolen credentials; IoT devices are common because they stay online. Defence includes secure defaults, patching, network segmentation and outbound anomaly detection. Disruption targets command infrastructure and infected hosts, while DDoS victims still need traffic absorption and filtering.

How do you protect an expensive API endpoint?

Authenticate early, authorise before expensive work, validate input and rate-limit by user, API key, IP and tenant with sensible burst allowance. Cache safe results, cap query complexity and payload size, use queues and concurrency limits, and give expensive work an asynchronous job interface. Add cost-based quotas rather than counting every request equally. Monitor p95 resource cost, rejection rate and false positives, and provide higher limits through explicit plans.

Rate-limiting algorithms?

A fixed window is simple but permits bursts at boundaries. A sliding log is accurate but stores every request; a sliding-window counter approximates it more cheaply. A token bucket adds tokens at a steady rate and allows controlled bursts, while a leaky bucket releases work at a steady rate and smooths traffic. Choose by product behaviour, distribute state consistently, return 429 with retry guidance, and avoid relying on IP alone.

How does a CDN help with DDoS?

A CDN terminates traffic across a large distributed edge, caches content, hides the origin and filters malicious requests before they cross the origin link. Anycast spreads volumetric traffic and the provider can challenge bots or scrub protocol attacks. It is not automatic protection: lock the origin so clients cannot bypass the CDN, cache safely, tune rules, protect dynamic APIs separately and arrange capacity and response support before an incident.

What is ransom DDoS?

In ransom DDoS, criminals threaten or launch a denial-of-service attack and demand payment to stop or avoid it. Treat the message as an incident: preserve it, notify security, leadership, providers and appropriate law enforcement, activate DDoS protection, review origin exposure and rehearse failover. Do not trust proof traffic or a promise to stop. Payment decisions are legal and executive risk decisions, not a technical response plan.

Your first 15 minutes under attack?

Declare an incident, assign an incident lead and open one communication channel. Confirm user impact and attack type from edge, network and application metrics; preserve timestamps and samples. Contact the ISP or DDoS provider, enable the prepared mitigation profile, protect the origin and apply conservative rate limits to the most abusive traffic. Keep stakeholders updated, avoid risky live changes, and record each action so recovery and review are possible.

08Vulnerability testing

Vulnerability assessment vs penetration test?

A vulnerability assessment broadly identifies and prioritises weaknesses, often with scanners plus manual validation. A penetration test has a defined objective and actively chains weaknesses to demonstrate what an attacker could achieve. Assessment gives coverage; penetration testing gives evidence of exploitability and business impact. Both need written scope and authorisation, safe test rules, validated findings, remediation advice and a retest.

SAST vs DAST vs IAST vs SCA?

SAST inspects source or bytecode early without running the app. DAST tests a running application from outside and finds observable behaviour. IAST instruments the running app during tests to connect requests with internal code paths. SCA inventories third-party components and known risks. Run fast SAST and SCA on pull requests, deeper tests in CI, DAST against a staging environment, and triage results with context rather than treating every scanner alert as true.

Types of SQL injection and blind injection?

In-band injection returns data through the same response, using errors or UNION queries. Blind injection gives no direct data, so the attacker infers truth from a changed response or timing delay. Out-of-band injection causes the database to contact another system when supported. These are observation methods, not excuses to test production destructively. Prevent all variants with parameterised queries, safe stored procedures, allowlisted identifiers and least-privilege database accounts.

How do prepared statements prevent injection?

A prepared statement sends SQL structure separately from parameter values. The database parses the command with placeholders, then binds input as typed data, so characters inside a value cannot become SQL syntax. Parameters usually cannot represent table names or sort directions; those dynamic identifiers need an allowlist. Prepared statements do not fix unsafe SQL built earlier, excessive database privilege or injection into a different interpreter.

Can an ORM still be vulnerable?

Yes. Normal ORM filters bind values safely, but raw-query APIs, string-built clauses and user-controlled column or order expressions can reintroduce injection. For example, concatenating a request parameter into ORDER BY is unsafe even if the search value is bound. Use ORM parameters, map user choices to known field names, review raw SQL, keep database privileges narrow and test generated queries at trust boundaries.

What is second-order SQL injection?

Second-order injection stores malicious-looking input safely at first, then a later process reads it and concatenates it into a new SQL statement. The original insert may be parameterised, so validation at entry alone misses the issue. Treat stored data as untrusted whenever it crosses into a new interpreter: parameterise every query at execution time, avoid dynamic SQL, allowlist identifiers and review background jobs and administrative tools too.

What are the three XSS types and how do you prevent them?

Stored XSS persists a payload; reflected XSS returns request input in a response; DOM XSS is created by unsafe client-side DOM operations. Prevent them with context-aware output encoding, safe templating and DOM APIs such as textContent, sanitisation only when HTML is intentionally allowed, and a restrictive Content Security Policy as backup. Avoid inserting untrusted text into HTML, JavaScript, CSS or URLs without the correct treatment.

What is CSRF, and how does it differ from XSS?

CSRF tricks a logged-in browser into sending an unwanted state-changing request because the browser automatically includes credentials. XSS runs attacker-controlled script in the trusted site and can often defeat CSRF protections. Defend CSRF with unpredictable tokens, SameSite cookies, origin checks and reauthentication for sensitive actions. Defend XSS with output encoding, safe DOM use and CSP. State-changing operations should not use GET.

What is IDOR and how do you test it?

An insecure direct object reference occurs when changing an object identifier lets a user access another user’s resource because server-side authorisation is missing. With written permission, create two test accounts and try reading or changing each other’s objects through URLs, APIs and nested resources. The fix is an ownership or policy check on every request; unguessable IDs reduce enumeration but are not authorisation.

A critical production vulnerability: next 24 hours?

Validate it safely, open an incident and restrict details to the response team. Assess exposure and evidence of exploitation, preserve logs, apply the fastest reversible containment such as disabling a feature or tightening access, then develop and test the permanent fix. Rotate affected secrets, notify required stakeholders and deploy with monitoring. Retest, document timeline and impact, meet legal disclosure duties, and run a blameless root-cause review.

09TLS interception

Walk through the TLS handshake.

In TLS 1.3 the client sends supported versions, cipher suites, a key share and random data. The server selects parameters, returns its key share and certificate, and signs the transcript to prove possession of the certificate key. The client validates the certificate and signature. Both sides derive symmetric session keys from the key exchange, exchange finished messages that authenticate the transcript, and then encrypt application data. Session resumption can shorten later handshakes.

What can an attacker see on public Wi-Fi when you use HTTPS?

They can usually see IP addresses, timing, connection sizes and DNS names unless encrypted DNS is used; the destination hostname may also be exposed through DNS or TLS metadata. They cannot normally read HTTP paths, headers, cookies or bodies, or modify them without causing certificate errors. HTTPS does not protect a compromised device, a malicious endpoint, phishing, or traffic after the server decrypts it.

What is SNI and why can it leak a hostname?

Server Name Indication lets a TLS client tell a shared server which hostname it wants so the server can choose the right certificate. In traditional TLS that ClientHello field is visible to network observers even though later HTTP data is encrypted. Encrypted ClientHello can conceal it when the client, DNS setup and server support it. IP address and traffic patterns may still reveal the service.

What is certificate pinning?

Pinning makes an application accept only a chosen certificate or public key in addition to normal trust validation. It can reduce interception through a compromised or user-installed CA, which may suit controlled high-risk mobile apps. It also creates outage risk during certificate rotation and complicates debugging. Use backup pins, a rotation plan and remote recovery; browsers generally rely on the public PKI plus controls such as Certificate Transparency.

How does a browser trust a certificate?

The browser builds a chain from the site certificate through intermediates to a trusted root in its store. It verifies signatures, hostname, validity dates, key usage and policy constraints, and may check revocation information and Certificate Transparency requirements. A valid chain proves control of the certified name under that CA system; it does not prove the site is honest. Time errors, name mismatch or an unknown issuer trigger warnings.

What is HSTS?

HTTP Strict Transport Security is a response header telling browsers to use HTTPS for a domain for a stated period, optionally including subdomains. It prevents protocol downgrade and cookie exposure through an initial HTTP redirect after the browser has learned the policy. Preloading can protect the very first visit. HSTS does not fix certificate errors or application vulnerabilities, and a careless long-lived subdomain policy can cause outages.

What is forward secrecy?

Forward secrecy means compromising a server’s long-term private key later does not decrypt previously recorded sessions. Ephemeral Diffie–Hellman key exchange creates a fresh session secret that is not recoverable from the certificate key. This is why possessing a server key is generally insufficient to passively decrypt modern TLS captures. Authorised debugging instead uses endpoint session-key logging or a controlled TLS interception proxy.

How do you inspect a mobile app that refuses your proxy?

Only test an app you own or are authorised to assess. First use a debug build that trusts the test CA and can disable pinning through a build flag; configure the emulator’s proxy and inspect traffic in a controlled lab. If the production app pins certificates, ask developers for an instrumented test build rather than weakening a real user build. Server logs and mocked networking are safer alternatives for many debugging cases.

What is a self-signed certificate?

A self-signed certificate is signed by its own private key rather than by a CA the browser already trusts. It can still provide encryption, but the browser cannot independently bind that key to the claimed hostname, so it warns about possible impersonation. It is reasonable in a controlled internal or development environment when its root is distributed securely; public services should use a publicly trusted CA.

How would you detect TLS interception?

Inspect the certificate chain and issuer from a known-clean network and compare it with the suspect path; an enterprise or unknown root appearing only on one device is a clue. Monitor unexpected trust-store changes, proxy settings, DNS changes and certificate-transparency records. Applications can use pinning where its operational risks are justified. Remember that authorised corporate inspection may be intentional, so confirm policy before treating it as compromise.

10HTTP and HTTPS

SSL vs TLS?

SSL is the obsolete predecessor of TLS. SSL 2.0 and 3.0 are insecure, and modern systems should use TLS 1.2 or 1.3. People still say “SSL certificate,” but the certificate is used by TLS. Disable old protocol versions and weak cipher suites, keep libraries current and test configuration. The security comes from the protocol and correct validation, not from the label on the certificate.

Why does TLS use symmetric and asymmetric cryptography?

Public-key cryptography authenticates the server and helps establish a shared secret without a prior secret; it is relatively expensive. TLS derives symmetric session keys from that exchange and uses them to encrypt and authenticate bulk data efficiently. Modern handshakes commonly use ephemeral Diffie–Hellman for key agreement and a certificate signature for identity. This hybrid provides scalable authentication, speed and forward secrecy.

What is a digital certificate and what does a CA do?

A certificate binds a public key to identities such as domain names and includes validity and usage information. A certificate authority validates control according to its policy and signs that binding. Browsers trust selected CA roots and validate the chain during TLS. The CA does not encrypt the user’s traffic or guarantee that the site is ethical; it vouches for the certified identity under the public-key infrastructure.

What is a digital signature?

The signer hashes the message and uses a private signing key to create a signature. A verifier uses the corresponding public key to confirm that the signature matches the message, proving integrity and possession of the private key. Identity still depends on how that public key was trusted, such as through a certificate. Signatures do not hide the message; encryption is a separate operation.

Does HTTPS prevent phishing?

No. HTTPS authenticates the domain in the address bar and protects traffic to it; a phisher can register a convincing different domain and obtain a valid certificate. Users still need to verify the domain and request, while organisations use password managers, phishing-resistant MFA, email controls, browser protections and rapid takedown. The padlock means the connection is encrypted, not that the business behind the site is trustworthy.

Why is HTTP stateless, and how do sessions work?

Each HTTP request is independent; the protocol does not automatically remember earlier requests. A server creates session state and sends the browser a random session identifier in a cookie, which the browser returns on later requests. Alternatively a signed token carries claims. Protect cookies with Secure, HttpOnly and suitable SameSite settings, rotate IDs after login, expire sessions and keep sensitive server state out of client-controlled data.

Main HTTP methods; PUT vs PATCH?

GET reads, POST submits or creates, PUT replaces a resource at a known URI, PATCH applies a partial change, and DELETE removes. HEAD returns headers without a body; OPTIONS describes communication options. PUT is intended to be idempotent: repeating the same replacement has the same result. PATCH can be idempotent depending on its operation format. Servers must still enforce authentication, authorisation and validation for every method.

What do 301, 302, 401, 403, 429 and 502 mean?

301 is a permanent redirect; 302 is a temporary redirect. 401 means authentication is missing or invalid, while 403 means the server understood the identity but refuses access. 429 means too many requests and should usually include retry guidance. 502 means a gateway or proxy received an invalid response from an upstream service. Client behaviour, caching and method preservation can differ among redirect codes.

What is CORS?

Cross-Origin Resource Sharing is a browser mechanism by which a server declares which other origins may read its responses. Browsers send an Origin header and sometimes a preflight OPTIONS request; the server returns allowed origins, methods and headers. CORS is not authentication and does not stop curl or server-to-server requests. Avoid reflecting arbitrary origins, especially with credentials, and authorise the API normally.

HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 improvements?

HTTP/2 multiplexes streams over one TCP connection, compresses headers and uses binary framing, reducing connection overhead; a lost TCP packet can still stall all streams. HTTP/3 carries HTTP over QUIC on UDP, so loss on one stream does not block the others and connection setup and migration improve. Neither version changes HTTP semantics or removes the need for caching, secure application logic and careful performance measurement.

11Cryptography

Symmetric vs asymmetric encryption?

Symmetric encryption uses one shared secret and is fast, so AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 protects files and network payloads. Asymmetric cryptography uses a public/private key pair and supports key agreement, suitable encryption schemes and signatures, but costs more. Real systems combine them: public-key methods authenticate and establish a session secret, then symmetric authenticated encryption protects the data.

Hashing vs encryption vs encoding?

Encryption is reversible with a key and provides confidentiality. A cryptographic hash maps data to a fixed-size digest and is designed to be one-way and collision-resistant; it supports integrity checks, signatures and password schemes. Encoding such as Base64 only changes representation so data can be transported; anyone can reverse it. Never describe encoding or unsalted password hashing as encryption.

Why is MD5 broken? What is a collision?

A collision is two different inputs with the same hash. Practical chosen-prefix collisions exist for MD5, so an attacker can construct different files that share a digest; MD5 cannot safely protect signatures or integrity against an adversary. Use SHA-256 or SHA-3 for general hashing and HMAC when a secret key is needed. For passwords, use a slow password-hashing function rather than a fast general hash.

What is salting, and why does it defeat rainbow tables?

A salt is a unique random value stored with each password hash. The password-hashing function processes password plus salt, so identical passwords have different stored results and precomputed rainbow tables would need rebuilding for every salt. Salts need not be secret. They do not make fast hashes safe; use Argon2id, scrypt, bcrypt or PBKDF2 with an appropriate cost, and optionally protect a separate server-side pepper.

How would you store user passwords?

Use a vetted library with Argon2id where available, a unique random salt and a memory/time cost tuned to the server. Store the algorithm, parameters, salt and output, never plaintext or reversible encryption. Compare in constant time, rate-limit login and add MFA. Rehash after successful login when parameters become outdated, protect password resets as carefully as login, and consider a pepper stored outside the database.

How is a digital signature created and verified?

The signer hashes the exact message and applies a signature algorithm with the private key. The verifier hashes the received message and verifies the signature with the public key. A valid result shows the content was not changed and the signer possessed the private key; trust in the person or service comes from a certificate or another key-distribution method. Use modern libraries and algorithms, not hand-built crypto.

What is a nonce or IV, and what if it is reused?

A nonce or initialisation vector makes repeated encryption under one key produce safe, distinct ciphertexts. Requirements depend on the mode: with AES-GCM a nonce must be unique per key, while some schemes require unpredictability. Reuse can reveal relationships between plaintexts and, in GCM, can enable authentication forgeries. Generate it with the library, store or transmit it with the ciphertext, and rotate keys before counters repeat.

How do you store API keys and application secrets?

Keep secrets in a managed secret store or platform identity system, encrypted at rest and delivered at runtime—not in source code, images or ordinary logs. Give each service a separate least-privilege identity, prefer short-lived credentials, restrict who and what can read them, and audit access. Rotate automatically, scan repositories and build outputs for leaks, and revoke immediately when exposure is suspected.

What is end-to-end encryption, and how does WhatsApp use it?

End-to-end encryption means only communicating endpoints hold the keys needed to read message content; the service transports ciphertext. WhatsApp uses the Signal protocol design, with per-device identity and session keys, forward secrecy and ongoing key updates. Users can verify security codes. Metadata and endpoint security remain concerns, and cloud backups need their own protection; E2EE cannot help if a participant’s device is compromised.

What threat does quantum computing pose?

A sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor’s algorithm could break widely used RSA and elliptic-curve public-key systems. Grover’s algorithm weakens symmetric brute-force security roughly quadratically, which larger keys can offset. The present concern is “harvest now, decrypt later” for long-lived data. Inventory cryptography, design crypto agility and migrate according to standardised post-quantum algorithms and vendor guidance rather than inventing schemes.

12SQL foundations

DELETE vs TRUNCATE vs DROP?

DELETE removes selected rows and can use WHERE; it normally logs row changes and fires delete behaviour. TRUNCATE removes all rows through a database-specific DDL operation that is usually faster and may reset identity values. DROP removes the table object, data and definition. Transaction, trigger and foreign-key behaviour varies by database, so state the product when discussing rollback details.

DDL, DML, DCL and TCL?

DDL defines database objects: CREATE, ALTER and DROP. DML reads or changes rows: SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE. DCL manages privileges through GRANT and REVOKE. TCL controls transactions through COMMIT, ROLLBACK and SAVEPOINT. Exact classification and implicit-commit behaviour can differ among database products.

Explain ACID.

Atomicity makes a transaction all-or-nothing; a transfer cannot debit without crediting. Consistency preserves declared rules such as nonnegative balance and foreign keys. Isolation controls what concurrent transactions observe, preventing anomalies according to the chosen level. Durability means committed results survive failure through logging and recovery. ACID does not mean every business rule is automatic—the schema and transaction code must express those rules correctly.

Transactions, COMMIT, ROLLBACK and SAVEPOINT?

A transaction groups operations into one logical unit. COMMIT makes its changes permanent and visible according to isolation; ROLLBACK abandons uncommitted changes. A SAVEPOINT marks a point to which part of the transaction can roll back without discarding earlier work. Keep transactions short, handle errors explicitly and avoid user interaction while locks are held. Autocommit and DDL rules depend on the database.

Find the second-highest salary.

If “second-highest” means the second distinct salary, use DENSE_RANK() OVER (ORDER BY salary DESC) in a subquery and filter for rank 2. To return employees too, rank the original rows rather than only distinct salary values. This handles ties clearly. State how NULL salaries should be treated and use a deterministic secondary order only when the question asks for one employee.

WHERE vs HAVING?

WHERE filters input rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after GROUP BY. For example, WHERE status='active' selects active employees before calculating each department’s count, while HAVING COUNT(*) > 10 keeps departments with more than ten. Put ordinary row conditions in WHERE for clarity and efficiency; use HAVING for aggregate conditions.

INNER, LEFT, RIGHT and FULL OUTER JOIN?

An inner join returns matching rows from both sides. A left join returns every left row plus matches, filling missing right columns with NULL; a right join is the mirror image. A full outer join returns all matched and unmatched rows from both tables. For departments and employees, use a left join to include empty departments. Conditions on the optional side often belong in ON, or a WHERE filter can accidentally remove NULL rows.

What is an index?

An index is an auxiliary structure, commonly a B-tree, that lets the database locate rows without scanning the whole table and can support ordering or uniqueness. It costs storage and makes inserts, updates and deletes maintain another structure. Index columns used selectively in filters, joins and ordering, considering the leftmost-prefix rule for composite indexes. Verify with the query plan; too many or poorly ordered indexes can hurt.

Explain 1NF, 2NF and 3NF.

First normal form gives each field one value and removes repeating groups. Second normal form is 1NF plus no non-key attribute depending on only part of a composite key. Third normal form is 2NF plus no non-key attribute depending transitively on another non-key attribute. Normalisation reduces duplicate facts and update anomalies; deliberate denormalisation may improve measured read performance, but the consistency cost should be explicit.

Clustered vs non-clustered index?

A clustered index determines how table rows are organised at the leaf level, so a table generally has one such ordering. A non-clustered index is a separate structure whose leaves contain keys plus a row locator and optional included columns; a table can have many. Range scans often benefit from clustering, while non-clustered indexes support other access paths. Terminology and physical implementation vary across SQL Server, MySQL and PostgreSQL.

13Database keys

Candidate key, super key and alternate key?

A super key is any set of columns that uniquely identifies a row, even if it contains unnecessary columns. A candidate key is a minimal super key: remove any component and it no longer guarantees uniqueness. One candidate key is chosen as the primary key; the remaining candidate keys are alternate keys and should normally receive unique constraints. For a user, internal ID and verified email might both be candidates.

Can primary and foreign keys be NULL?

A primary key cannot be NULL because it must identify every row uniquely. A foreign key can usually be NULL when the relationship is optional; NULL means no referenced row, and the constraint does not require a match. Declare it NOT NULL when every child must have a parent. Composite foreign keys and NULL treatment have product-specific details, so model optionality deliberately.

What is a composite key?

A composite key uses multiple columns together to identify a row. In enrolment(student_id, course_id), neither column is unique alone but the pair can be the primary key, naturally preventing duplicate enrolment. Use it when the combination is stable and meaningful. If many tables must reference a wide or changeable key, a surrogate ID plus a unique constraint on the natural combination can simplify relationships.

What is referential integrity?

Referential integrity ensures every non-NULL foreign-key value refers to an existing parent key and defines what happens when that parent changes or is deleted. Without it, orphan orders, invalid department IDs and inconsistent reports appear, especially under concurrent writes. Database constraints are stronger than application-only checks because every writer follows them. Choose restrict, cascade or set-null behaviour from the business lifecycle.

ON DELETE CASCADE and its risk?

ON DELETE CASCADE automatically deletes child rows when the parent is deleted. It fits tightly owned data such as order-item rows that have no meaning without an order. The risk is a mistaken parent deletion silently removing a large graph of valuable data. Avoid it across unclear ownership boundaries, test cascade depth, restrict deletion permissions, audit destructive operations and keep recoverable backups.

Unique key vs primary key?

Both enforce uniqueness. The primary key is the table’s chosen row identity, is implicitly NOT NULL and there is only one primary-key constraint, though it may contain several columns. A table can have several unique constraints for alternate identifiers. Whether a unique constraint permits one or multiple NULLs varies by database. Foreign keys can often reference a suitably constrained unique candidate key as well as the primary key.

Can a table have two primary or foreign keys?

A table has one primary-key constraint, but that key may be composite. It can have any practical number of foreign-key constraints, including multiple columns referencing the same parent for different roles—for example created_by and approved_by both referencing users. Name relationships clearly, index commonly joined foreign-key columns and define optionality and deletion behaviour for each one.

How do you model many-to-many?

Create an associative table with foreign keys to both parents. For students and courses, enrolment(student_id, course_id, enrolled_at, grade) represents the relationship and can use the pair as its primary key or a unique constraint. Relationship attributes belong there. This turns one many-to-many relationship into two one-to-many relationships and lets the database enforce that both referenced rows exist.

Surrogate vs natural key?

A natural key has business meaning, such as an ISO code; a surrogate key is generated only for identity, such as a bigint or UUID. I prefer a surrogate when the natural value is long, mutable, sensitive or controlled externally, while still enforcing the natural candidate with a unique constraint. A short, stable, universally defined natural key can be fine. Key choice should consider change, joins, distribution and exposure.

Can a foreign key reference the same table?

Yes; a self-referencing foreign key models hierarchies and relationships among rows of one type. An employee table can have nullable manager_id referencing employee_id, with NULL for the top level. Prevent invalid cycles when the domain forbids them, choose deletion behaviour carefully, and use recursive CTEs to traverse the hierarchy. The foreign-key constraint alone prevents missing parents but not arbitrary cycles.

Explain the SDLC and where testing fits.

The lifecycle covers discovery and requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, operation and eventual retirement. Testing begins before coding: clarify acceptance criteria, review design risks, write unit and integration tests during implementation, run system, security and performance checks before release, then monitor production and turn incidents into regression tests. Agile; Scrum vs Kanban?

Scrum organises work into time-boxed sprints with defined accountabilities and events such as planning, review and retrospective. 15OOP design

Model a car, bank account, library or ATM.

Start from responsibilities and invariants, not nouns alone. For a bank account: Account owns balance and transaction history, exposes deposit and withdraw, and prevents invalid amounts or overdraft according to policy. Customer owns accounts; a TransferService coordinates two accounts atomically; repositories handle persistence. Keep money as decimal or integer minor units and make operations idempotent.

Design an e-commerce product catalogue hierarchy.

Use one Product entity for shared identity, title, description and brand, then separate sellable Variant objects for SKU, price and stock. Categories form a hierarchy and products relate to many categories. Type-specific properties belong in validated attribute definitions or composed detail objects, not dozens of fragile subclasses. Services handle search, pricing and inventory because those behaviours span products and change independently.

When do you use composition instead of inheritance?

Prefer composition when the relationship is “has a,” behaviour must vary at runtime, or subclasses would inherit state and methods they cannot honour. A car has an engine; a notification service has a delivery strategy. Inheritance is suitable for a stable “is a” relationship where every subtype obeys the parent contract. Composition reduces coupling and combinatorial subclass trees, though it may require more delegation code.

Four OOP pillars with project examples?

Encapsulation keeps an order’s state private and changes it through validated methods. Abstraction exposes PaymentGateway.charge without gateway details. Inheritance can share a sound contract among specialised report types, though composition is often safer. Polymorphism lets checkout call the same gateway interface for card or wallet implementations. Use examples you built and explain the problem each idea solved, rather than only definitions.

Abstraction vs encapsulation?

Abstraction presents the essential operation while hiding unnecessary implementation detail: callers use send(message) without knowing SMTP. Encapsulation keeps state and behaviour together and controls access so invariants hold: a BankAccount does not expose a freely writable balance. Interfaces and modules support abstraction; private fields and carefully designed methods support encapsulation. A class can provide one without doing the other well.

Why can inheritance create tight coupling?

A subclass depends on the parent’s public and protected behaviour, construction rules and sometimes internal assumptions. A parent change can unexpectedly break many descendants, and a deep hierarchy makes behaviour hard to trace. Subclasses may also inherit irrelevant methods. Keep hierarchies shallow, depend on explicit contracts, protect invariants and prefer composition for reusable behaviour. Inheritance is safest when the subtype relationship is stable and substitutable.

Explain Liskov substitution with a violation.

A subtype must be usable wherever its base type is expected without breaking the base contract. The classic violation is Square extends Rectangle when callers set width and height independently; forcing them equal changes expected behaviour. Model both as separate shapes implementing area, or make dimensions immutable with honest contracts. A subtype must not strengthen preconditions, weaken promised results or violate invariants.

What are the SOLID principles?

Single responsibility: one reason to change. Open/closed: extend behaviour through stable contracts rather than repeatedly editing central logic. Liskov substitution: subtypes preserve the parent contract. Interface segregation: clients depend only on operations they need. Dependency inversion: high-level policy depends on abstractions, not concrete infrastructure. They are design heuristics, not a demand for one class per method; apply them where they reduce change cost.

Design a parking-lot system.

Core objects are ParkingLot, floors, spots with type and state, gates, tickets, vehicles and payments. A SpotAssignmentStrategy chooses a compatible free spot; a PricingPolicy calculates fees. Entry atomically reserves a spot and issues a ticket; exit calculates, takes an idempotent payment and frees the spot. Separate display and persistence, define concurrency rules, and ask whether reservations, multiple vehicle sizes and lost tickets are in scope.

What design patterns have you used?

Name only patterns tied to real problems. “I used Strategy to select pricing rules without conditionals, Factory to create a provider from configuration, Adapter to place two payment SDKs behind one interface, and Observer for domain-event subscribers.” Explain the trade-off: patterns add indirection, so a simple function may be better until variation exists. Also distinguish dependency injection—a wiring technique—from the service-locator anti-pattern.

16Advanced SQL

Find the second-highest or Nth-highest marks without LIMIT.

Rank distinct scores with DENSE_RANK() OVER (ORDER BY marks DESC) and filter the outer query for the requested rank. That returns all ties at the Nth distinct mark. Without window functions, select a score where exactly N−1 distinct scores are greater using a correlated subquery, though it is harder to read and often slower. Define whether “Nth” counts duplicate scores before writing the query.

Students above their department average?

A clear window-function solution calculates AVG(marks) OVER (PARTITION BY department_id) for every student in a subquery, then filters marks > dept_avg outside it. A correlated subquery can calculate the average for the current student’s department but may repeat work. State the subject and NULL rules, and include student, department, marks and average in the output so the result is easy to verify.

Departments where no student scored above 90?

Use NOT EXISTS: select each department for which no related marks row has score > 90. This naturally includes departments with no students if the requirement says “no student”; if empty departments should be excluded, add an EXISTS check for a student. Conditional aggregation is another option: group departments and require the count of scores above 90 to equal zero.

Department student counts, including zero?

Start from departments, left join students on department ID, group by the department key and name, and use COUNT(s.student_id). Counting the non-NULL student key produces zero for an unmatched department; COUNT(*) would incorrectly count the placeholder row as one. Put filters on students in the ON clause when empty departments must remain.

Find duplicates and delete while keeping one.

First group by the columns that define a duplicate and use HAVING COUNT(*) > 1. To remove extras, assign ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY those_columns ORDER BY created_at, id) and delete rows where the number exceeds 1, using the database’s supported CTE/delete syntax. Preview the exact IDs in a transaction, back up if needed, and add a unique constraint afterward to stop recurrence.

RANK vs DENSE_RANK vs ROW_NUMBER?

For scores 100, 100, 90: ROW_NUMBER produces 1, 2, 3 using a tie-breaker; RANK produces 1, 1, 3 and leaves a gap; DENSE_RANK produces 1, 1, 2 without a gap. Use row number when exactly one ordered position is needed, rank for competition ranking, and dense rank for Nth distinct value. Add a deterministic secondary order when row number chooses among ties.

WHERE vs HAVING with GROUP BY?

WHERE removes rows before aggregation; HAVING removes groups after aggregation. To find departments with at least five active students, use WHERE student.status='active', group by department, then HAVING COUNT(*) >= 5. Using HAVING for a nonaggregate condition can be unclear and may do extra work. Be careful with outer joins because a WHERE condition can remove unmatched rows.

Correlated subquery vs join—which is faster?

Pre-aggregate the inner table once by its join key, then join that result to the outer table. This often avoids conceptually repeating a lookup for each outer row and can be clearer. Modern optimisers may decorrelate the original query into the same plan, so neither syntax is universally faster. Compare actual plans and data volumes, ensure join keys are indexed, and preserve semantics around duplicates and NULLs.

View vs CTE vs temporary table?

A view is a stored query definition reused across statements and can provide a security boundary; it usually does not store results. A CTE names a query result within one statement and improves composition or recursion, but is not automatically materialised. A temporary table stores intermediate rows for a session or transaction, can be indexed and reused, but costs writes and cleanup. Choose based on lifetime, reuse, optimisation and data size.

Students enrolled in every subject?

This is relational division. One solution groups enrolments by student and keeps students whose count of distinct subject IDs equals the total subject count. A more exact pattern selects students for whom there does not exist a subject for which no matching enrolment exists—double NOT EXISTS. The latter handles the logical “every” directly. Clarify which subjects are required and what an empty subject set should mean.

17Java and OOP

Four OOP pillars with examples?

Encapsulation protects an account balance behind validated methods. Abstraction exposes a PaymentGateway contract without provider details. Inheritance lets a sound subtype reuse and specialise a parent contract. Polymorphism lets checkout call charge on any gateway implementation. The useful interview answer connects each definition to a design consequence and acknowledges that composition often replaces inheritance when behaviour varies independently.

Why no multiple inheritance of classes in Java?

Multiple class inheritance creates ambiguity when two parents provide the same method or state, complicating construction and method resolution—the diamond problem. Java keeps one implementation superclass and allows multiple interfaces, giving multiple contracts without inheriting multiple object states. Interfaces may have default methods, but Java forces the implementing class to resolve conflicting defaults explicitly. Composition can combine independent implementations with clearer ownership.

Diamond problem and Java 8 default methods?

If interfaces A and B both define the same default method and class C implements both, Java rejects the ambiguity until C overrides it. The override may choose A.super.method(), B.super.method() or its own logic. A concrete superclass method takes precedence over interface defaults. Thus default methods allow interface evolution but retain explicit conflict resolution rather than silent multiple inheritance.

Can an abstract class have a constructor or no abstract methods?

Yes to both. An abstract class constructor initialises the inherited part of a subclass object and runs through super(...); the abstract class itself still cannot be instantiated. A class may be marked abstract with zero abstract methods to prevent direct construction or provide a controlled base implementation. Use an abstract class when shared state or protected implementation matters, and an interface for a lighter behavioural contract.

Can an interface have variables?

Interface fields are implicitly public static final, so they are constants and must be initialised; interfaces do not hold per-object instance state. Interface methods can be abstract, default, static and, in modern Java, private helpers with rules depending on the form. Avoid placing unrelated constants in an interface merely to import them—the constant-interface pattern leaks implementation details into a type contract.

Overloading vs overriding?

Overloading uses the same method name with different parameter lists and is chosen at compile time from the declared types and arguments. Overriding provides a subclass implementation with the same signature and compatible return type; runtime dispatch chooses it from the actual object. Return type alone cannot overload. Static methods are hidden rather than overridden, and private methods are not inherited for overriding.

Runtime polymorphism and dynamic dispatch?

A base-type reference can point to a subtype object, and an overridden instance-method call is selected at runtime from the object’s actual class. For example, Shape s = new Circle(); s.area() invokes Circle.area. This lets callers depend on a stable abstraction while implementations vary. Fields and static methods do not use the same dynamic dispatch, and constructors are not overridden.

What do final, static, this and super do?

final prevents reassignment of a variable, overriding of a method or extension of a class; a final object reference does not make the object immutable. static belongs to the class rather than an instance. this refers to the current object or another constructor in the same class. super accesses the immediate parent implementation or constructor. Constructor calls using this or super must come first.

Functional interface and lambda?

A functional interface has one abstract method, such as Predicate<T>.test, though it may also have default and static methods. A lambda supplies an implementation of that single method, so x -> x > 0 can be used as a predicate. @FunctionalInterface asks the compiler to enforce the rule. Captured local variables must be final or effectively final.

SOLID, especially interface segregation?

Interface segregation says clients should not depend on methods they do not use. Instead of one Machine interface with print, scan and fax that forces a basic printer to throw exceptions, create small Printer, Scanner and Fax contracts and compose capable devices. This complements single responsibility, open/closed, Liskov substitution and dependency inversion by keeping contracts focused and substitutable.

18Applied cybersecurity

XSS vs CSRF?

XSS makes attacker-controlled script run in a trusted site, through stored, reflected or DOM-based injection. CSRF makes a logged-in browser send an unwanted request using automatically attached credentials. Prevent XSS with context-aware output encoding, safe DOM APIs, sanitisation where HTML is allowed and CSP. Prevent CSRF with SameSite cookies, anti-CSRF tokens, origin checks and reauthentication. XSS can often bypass CSRF defences, so fixing XSS is fundamental.

How would you secure a REST API end to end?

Use TLS, strong authentication, short-lived tokens, server-side object and function authorisation, schema validation, parameterised data access and least privilege. Add rate and size limits, idempotency for writes, careful CORS, safe error messages, secret management and encryption for sensitive stored data. Log security events with correlation IDs, monitor anomalies, patch dependencies, test access-control cases in CI and maintain an incident and key-rotation plan.

What is a JWT and its common pitfalls?

A JSON Web Token is a compact set of claims protected by a signature or MAC; it is usually signed, not encrypted. Verify the expected algorithm, signature, issuer, audience, expiry and token type, and never accept an algorithm selected unsafely by input. Keep tokens short-lived, avoid sensitive payloads, protect storage from XSS and plan revocation or key rotation. JWT does not replace authorisation checks or server-side session needs.

OAuth 2.0 vs SSO vs SAML?

OAuth 2.0 delegates limited API access: a client obtains an access token for a resource server. OpenID Connect adds authentication and identity claims on top of OAuth. SSO is the user experience of authenticating once across applications, implemented with protocols such as OIDC or SAML. SAML is an XML-based federation protocol common in enterprise browser SSO. Use authorisation code with PKCE for modern user-facing OAuth clients.

What does HTTPS protect and not protect?

HTTPS uses TLS to authenticate the server and protect HTTP confidentiality and integrity in transit between endpoints. It does not secure a compromised browser or server, stop phishing on a different valid domain, fix broken access control, hide all metadata, or protect data after either endpoint decrypts it. Pair it with secure cookies, HSTS, application controls, endpoint security, safe storage and monitoring.

Firewall vs IDS vs IPS?

A firewall allows or blocks traffic using network, transport or application rules. An intrusion detection system observes activity and alerts on suspicious patterns but is not normally inline. An intrusion prevention system is inline and can block or modify traffic, which adds false-positive and availability risk. These controls complement endpoint, identity and application security; tune them from real traffic, keep signatures current and send useful events to central monitoring.

Phishing and organisational defence?

Phishing uses deceptive messages or sites to steal credentials, deliver malware or trigger fraudulent actions. Use layered defence: secure email configuration and authentication, URL and attachment scanning, password managers, phishing-resistant MFA, patched endpoints, limited privilege and easy reporting. Train with realistic examples without blaming users. For payments and account changes, require an independent verification channel. Measure report rate and response time, not only simulated click rate.

Ransomware recovery plan?

Prepare offline or immutable tested backups, segmented networks, MFA, EDR, least privilege and a documented response plan. During an incident, isolate affected systems without destroying evidence, disable compromised accounts, determine scope and entry path, engage legal and response teams, and protect unaffected backups. Rebuild from known-good images, rotate credentials, restore in business-priority order and monitor. Recovery is proven by regular restore exercises, not by owning backup files.

Production server compromised—what do you do?

Declare an incident, assign leadership, preserve volatile and durable evidence, and establish scope from trusted telemetry. Isolate the host or workload and block attacker access while maintaining evidence; rotate affected credentials from clean systems. Find the entry path and persistence, rebuild from trusted artefacts rather than merely deleting suspicious files, patch the cause, restore carefully and increase monitoring. Meet notification duties and document a timeline and lessons.

Security in a CI/CD pipeline?

Protect branches and reviews, use isolated short-lived runners and workload identity instead of long-lived secrets, pin and scan dependencies, run SAST, secret scanning, tests and infrastructure-policy checks, and produce a software bill of materials. Sign artefacts and verify provenance at deployment, restrict who can promote releases, separate environments and scan deployed systems too. Block high-confidence critical issues while giving teams a documented exception process to avoid bypass culture.

19Transfer learning

Feature extraction vs fine-tuning?

Feature extraction freezes the pretrained backbone and trains only a new task head. It is cheap, stable and useful with little labelled data or a similar source domain. Fine-tuning updates some or all pretrained weights, adapting features more strongly but needing more compute and regularisation. Start with a frozen baseline, then unfreeze later layers with a lower learning rate if validation results justify it.

Which layers would you freeze?

Early layers often learn general features, while later layers are more task-specific, so I initially freeze the backbone and train the head. If the target domain differs or performance plateaus, unfreeze the last block, use a smaller learning rate and progressively unfreeze while watching validation metrics. The choice is empirical: data size, domain shift, model architecture, memory and overfitting matter more than a fixed rule.

What is catastrophic forgetting?

Catastrophic forgetting occurs when adapting a model to new data overwrites useful pretrained behaviour. Reduce it with a lower learning rate, partial freezing, parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA, regularisation toward original weights, and a mixture or replay sample of old-domain data when available. Evaluate both the new task and important original capabilities; success on the target set alone can hide a serious regression.

When is training from scratch better?

Transfer can hurt when the source domain or input structure is very different, pretrained biases conflict with the target, the architecture is unsuitable, or licensing and privacy prevent use. With a very large high-quality target dataset, training a purpose-built smaller model may outperform adaptation. Compare against a from-scratch baseline, inspect per-slice errors and learning curves, and do not assume a larger pretrained model always wins.

What is LoRA?

Low-Rank Adaptation freezes the original weight matrices and learns small low-rank update matrices in selected layers. Because only a small fraction of parameters receives gradients and optimiser state, training and storage are much cheaper than full fine-tuning. Adapters can be swapped per task and sometimes merged for inference. Rank, target modules, data quality and evaluation still matter; it is efficient adaptation, not automatic accuracy.

Overfitting vs underfitting?

Underfitting means the model cannot capture the pattern: training and validation errors are both high. Overfitting means it memorises training detail: training error improves while validation error stops improving or worsens. Diagnose with learning curves and representative splits. Address underfitting with better features, capacity or training; address overfitting with more data, augmentation, regularisation, simpler models, early stopping and leak-free cross-validation.

Bias–variance trade-off?

High bias comes from assumptions that are too simple and causes systematic underfitting. High variance means predictions change too much with the training sample and causes overfitting. More flexible models often lower bias but raise variance. Use learning curves and cross-validation to choose capacity, then control variance with more data, regularisation, ensembling or simpler features. Irreducible noise sets a floor that neither can remove.

Supervised, unsupervised and self-supervised learning?

Supervised learning trains from input–label pairs, such as images with defect classes. Unsupervised learning finds structure without task labels, such as clustering customers. Self-supervised learning creates a prediction target from the data itself—masking words or predicting image patches—so a model learns useful representations before fine-tuning. Self-supervision is technically label-generating from raw data and has become a major pretraining approach.

What is data augmentation, and how do you choose it?

Augmentation creates label-preserving training variations to improve robustness. Choose transformations that match real variation: crops, flips or colour changes for suitable images; noise or time shifts for audio; cautious paraphrase for text. A flip is wrong when left and right carry the label, and strong colour change is wrong when colour defines the class. Apply only to training data and validate each policy on realistic held-out slices.

How do you evaluate an imbalanced classifier?

Use a stratified, leak-free split and report the confusion matrix, per-class precision, recall and F1 rather than accuracy alone. Precision–recall curves and average precision are often more informative than ROC when positives are rare. Choose the decision threshold from the business cost of false positives and false negatives, check probability calibration and evaluate important subgroups. Compare with simple baselines and inspect actual errors.

20Open-source contribution

Walk through your hardest contribution line by line.

Choose one small diff you understand deeply. Begin with the failing behaviour and a minimal reproduction, show the call path to the root cause, then explain each meaningful change and the regression test. Discuss an alternative you rejected and why, plus compatibility or performance concerns. Do not narrate punctuation. Keep the repository or diff available and be honest about lines suggested by a reviewer or tool.

How did you navigate an unfamiliar codebase?

I first ran the project and its tests, then read the contribution guide and architecture notes. I traced a real request or failing test from its public entry point using search, call hierarchy and debugger, sketched the few modules involved, and read nearby tests before changing code. I asked one focused maintainer question after gathering evidence. That produces a narrow mental model quickly without pretending to understand the whole repository.

How did you handle a rejected or rewritten PR?

I separated feedback on the code from judgement of me, asked what constraint I had missed, and restated the maintainer’s concern to confirm it. If their approach fit project conventions better, I updated the PR and recorded what I learned. If I disagreed, I supplied a small benchmark or test rather than arguing from preference. Maintainers own the project direction; a closed PR can still be a useful contribution and lesson.

How did you test the change?

I reproduced the bug with the smallest failing test, then added a regression test that failed before the fix and passed after it. I ran focused unit tests, the affected integration path, formatting and the project’s required suite, and manually checked the user-visible scenario. With more time I would name one concrete gap—perhaps another platform, concurrency case or performance test—along with its risk, not vaguely say “more testing.”

Explain fork, branch, rebase and PR.

Fork the upstream repository to my account, clone my fork, add the original as upstream, and create a focused feature branch. Commit the change and tests, fetch upstream and rebase the branch onto its current main branch, resolving and retesting conflicts. Push my branch and open a pull request against upstream with the problem, approach and validation. Address review with clear commits, then maintainers merge according to project policy.

How do you resolve a merge conflict?

Fetch the latest branches and start the intended merge or rebase. For each marked file, understand both changes and create the correct combined result; do not simply choose “ours” or “theirs.” Remove markers, run focused tests, stage the resolution and continue. If the conflict changes behaviour I contact the other author. Afterward run the wider suite and inspect the final diff, especially when rebasing rewrites commits.

What makes a PR easy to review?

Keep it small and focused, explain the concrete problem and resulting behaviour, link the issue, and avoid unrelated formatting. Include tests, screenshots or measurements appropriate to the change and state any risk or migration. Use clear names and commits, comment on non-obvious trade-offs, and respond to review without hiding changed scope. A reviewer should understand why the change exists before reading the code.

What does the project’s licence allow?

Answer for the actual repository after reading its licence. MIT is permissive: reuse, modification and distribution are allowed with copyright and licence notice, without warranty. Apache 2.0 is similarly permissive and includes an express patent grant and notice requirements. GPL is copyleft: distributed derivative works must generally provide corresponding source under the GPL. Mention that dependencies and contribution agreements may add obligations; do not guess legal terms.

What do you look for in code review?

I first check whether the change solves the stated problem, then correctness on normal, edge and failure paths; security and privacy boundaries; concurrency and data migration; tests; compatibility; performance; and maintainability. I distinguish blocking defects from optional suggestions, explain why a comment matters and offer an example when useful. I review the design and tests as seriously as syntax, and acknowledge good decisions without filling the review with noise.

What would you change if you rebuilt your own project?

Choose a real architectural decision and tie it to evidence: “I coupled request handling to the database, which made tests slow and retries unsafe. I would introduce a small service boundary, idempotent operations and integration tests around persistence.” Explain why the original choice was reasonable with what you knew, what signal exposed the limit, and how you would migrate incrementally. Avoid claiming you would rewrite everything.
